Demo 18 · The village

Five specialists, one child, and no votes

One learner turn arrives. Five roles form an opinion about it and three of them disagree. Nothing is averaged, nothing is summarised, and nobody votes: a precedence ladder is walked, the first tier that can resolve the conflict does, and everyone who lost files dissent into the record instead of being overwritten.

Arbitration, conflict detection and tier availability all computed The T0 check actually runs The five opinions are scripted
What this is

A single turn through a five-role village, with the arbitration exposed. Each role carries three properties that decide what it is allowed to win: its grounding, its model family, and the claim types it is role of record for. Everything downstream is a function of those three.

Why it matters

Three benchmarks say multi-agent debate does not reliably beat plain self-consistency, and the diagnosis is worth carrying: two instances of the same model are not independent minds — they share a prior, a training set and a failure mode. A crew earns its keep only when the members differ in grounding, evidence and authority. Otherwise it is one voice billed five times.

How to use it

The interesting move is destructive. Lift T2 above T1 and read the diagnosis the village then publishes: three roles agree on the wrong answer and one does not, so a judge selecting by weight of support picks the wrong one. It is the only line in the output that changes, which is how you know the ladder is doing work rather than decorating it. The homogeneity switch in §2 does worse — it deletes the disagreement.

1 · The turn

Learner · 14:061/2 + 1/3 = 2/5. I got this wrong last week too. Can you just give me the rule? I have a test on Friday.
One turn. Five claim types are latent in it — a fact, a diagnosis, a history, a help policy and a next action — and no single role has authority over more than one of them.

2 · The active set

Three to five roles per learner-hour. The economics permit about forty; the orchestration evidence permits three to five, and this turn activates five. Each opinion below is a typed claim, not a paragraph: a claim type, an assertion, and the grounding that produced it.

3 · The ladder

Tried in order. The first tier that can resolve a conflict does, and the tiers below it are never consulted. Move a tier and everything below recomputes.

4 · Arbitration

One block per claim type. Conflicts are detected by comparing assertions, not by asking anyone. The trace shows every tier that was tried and why it could or could not resolve.

Watch the seam when you lift T2. With the judge above scope precedence, the village publishes “looks like a careless mistake” in the same breath as “this is the fourth time in sixteen days” — a sentence pair no role would have written and that only an aggregator can produce. Three roles agreeing does not make them independent; they agreed because the wrong reading is the easy reading, which is what a shared prior is. The correct answer is still in the dissent log, which is the whole reason the log exists.

5 · What the village says

The turn it emits
Assembled from the winning claim of each type. Sentences are templated and labelled scripted; which sentence appears is entirely computed.
The dissent log
Append-only, attributed, with grounding attached. A correct minority answer that is voted away is unrecoverable; the same answer appended to the record is recoverable — by a later probe, a human reviewer, or an offline audit.

6 · The constraint this design is answering

ResultFinding
Smit et al., Should we be going MAD? Multi-agent debating systems in their current form do not reliably outperform other prompting strategies, such as self-consistency and ensembling over multiple reasoning paths.
Wang et al., Rethinking the Bounds of LLM Reasoning A single agent with strong prompts matches the best discussion approach across a wide range of reasoning tasks. Discussion won only where the prompt contained no demonstration.
Becker et al., Stay Focused Debate drifts away from the initial problem over turns, degrading performance on long reasoning chains.
Du et al. (2023) The positive result, and the one usually cited. It is one against three.

And model diversity is not a free fix either: Self-MoA found that aggregating a single top model beats mixing different models — +6.6% on AlpacaEval 2.0 — because mixing lowers average quality. The reconcilable reading, published here as unresolved: heterogeneity helps when the mechanism is adversarial and hurts when it is aggregative. Which is exactly the rule the ladder encodes — it permits debate and forbids synthesis.

What this proves / what it does not

Demonstrated, in code, on this page

Arbitration is a decidable procedure, not a personality. Conflict detection is set comparison over typed assertions. Tier availability is a predicate over role properties — T0 asks whether any claimant is executable, T2 asks whether a judge exists outside every proposer's model family, T3 asks whether the type is one a learner may adjudicate at all. Reorder the ladder and the published diagnosis changes, which means the ladder is load-bearing rather than decorative.

And dissent survives. Every losing claim is in the log with its author and its grounding. Nothing was averaged, and the minority answer — which on the diagnosis is the correct one — remains recoverable no matter which tier won.

Conditions of use

The opinions are scripted, and that is where the real risk lives. Whether five live agents would produce claims this cleanly typed — one type each, one assertion each, no hedging, no drift — is untested and Becker et al. suggests they would not. The arbitration logic is sound; the inputs it arbitrates over are the hard part, and this page hands them to it.

Heterogeneity is asserted here, not achieved. The page labels the roles with different families and different groundings, and only two of those labels are real: the Simulator's grounding is an executable check that actually runs, and the Archivist's is a stored record. The other three are prompts wearing different names, which is precisely the failure mode the benchmarks measured.

Nobody has measured whether this beats one good agent. Not once. The village is a design response to three negative results, not a result of its own, and the honest position is that a single well-prompted agent with a calculator attached is the control condition nobody has run.
