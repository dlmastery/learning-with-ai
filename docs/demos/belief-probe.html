Demo 16 · The mechanism

The question that separates what you believe from what is true

Every tutoring system asks a model “is this wrong, and why?” — an open judgement over an unbounded space, and the thing models are measurably worst at. This page never asks that question. It holds six enumerated beliefs about adding fractions, asks two of them where they disagree, and updates a posterior in closed form. The whole loop is a table lookup.

Search, Bayes and timings all computed 1,296-item space, enumerated exhaustively Population priors are design proposals
What this is

A belief modelled as an object rather than a label with a strength attached. Three methods: predict(item) returns a distribution over responses, discriminate(rival) returns the item on which two beliefs part company, and break() runs a belief against invariants until it contradicts itself.

Why it matters

Across 223 real tutoring domains no model beat chance at labelling an incorrect student action. That result closes one door and leaves another wide open: an argmax over precomputed distributions needs no judgement at all. It is available today, at any capability level, and it costs microseconds.

How to use it

Three things happen below, in this order: the library is shown, a probe is found rather than written, and your answer moves a posterior. The number worth watching is not the verdict — it is the microsecond count under §3, because that is what makes the mechanism sit on the real-time path.

1 · The library

Six enumerated beliefs about a/b + c/d. Five are documented errors; one is the canonical procedure. Nothing outside this set can be diagnosed — which is the honest cost of the approach and the reason it is fast.

Note what is missing from that list. There is no “misconception detector”, no classifier, no prompt. Each belief is executable: given an item it returns an answer. That single property is what turns diagnosis from a judgement into a lookup.

2 · discriminate() — the probe is found, not authored

Pick any two beliefs. The system scans the entire item space, throws away every item on which the two agree, and among the survivors chooses the one that maximises expected information gain over the whole posterior. It is a search, and the search is shown.

Partial equivalence is the interesting output. Several pairs in this library are indistinguishable on most of the space — “multiply the bottoms” and “use the lcm” give identical answers on 788 of the 1,296 items — 60.8% of the space. A diagnostic written by hand would land in that region most of the time and learn nothing. The search will not.

3 · The probe loop

The two beliefs currently leading the posterior are asked for their separating item. You answer it. The posterior updates by Bayes. Repeat. At no point is anything asked whether your answer is wrong.

The line that carries the architecture

The belief object never asks whether an answer is wrong. It asks which of its enumerated beliefs predicted that answer. Chance-level open-ended judgement becomes a table lookup over a bounded set — and the library is the verifier, not a model that has learned to be one.

4 · break() — running a belief until it contradicts itself

The third method takes no rival. It takes the belief alone and searches a case space for a pair of instances the belief itself cannot reconcile. Four invariants, checked exhaustively. A belief that survives all four returns ⊥ — no contradiction found — which is what the correct procedure does.

Why break() matters more than being told. A counterexample generated from the learner's own stated rule closes the escape route “that isn't what I meant”. And the measured warrant for the whole move is narrower than the folklore: prediction has no main effect — the entire effect is the expectancy-violation interaction, p = .002. Predicting and being right does approximately nothing.

What this proves / what it does not

Read this table rather than a summary. The left column is what the page executed in front of you; the right column is what would still have to be true in a deployed system.

ClaimDemonstrated hereStill open
Diagnosis can avoid judgement entirely Yes — every verdict on this page is an argmax over precomputed distributions. No classifier, no model, no prompt anywhere on the path. Only for errors inside the library. A learner holding a seventh belief is diagnosed as the nearest of six, confidently and wrongly.
The probe can be found rather than written Yes, and the search reports its own blind spots: it prints how much of the space cannot separate the two beliefs at all. That the found item is askable — well-formed, in the learner's zone, and not pathological. Nothing here checks readability or difficulty.
It runs at interaction speed Yes, and the timings are measured on your machine, not quoted. The posterior update is the cheap part by three orders of magnitude. Nothing. This one is settled. Enumerated Bayes over ≤40 hypotheses will not be the bottleneck in any system anyone builds.
Enumerating beliefs is feasible for a real curriculum Not shown. Six beliefs for one operation took an afternoon of reading. The scaling cost. Fraction addition is the best-documented misconception space in education; most topics have no such literature, and the library would have to be mined from learner data rather than papers.
The learner changes their mind Not shown, and not shown anywhere else either. Everything. Cross-ontological errors do not decay: the Bohr-model hybrid population was unchanged across a full semester. Correct diagnosis is upstream of repair, not a substitute for it.
One design consequence worth carrying. Because misconceptions do not decay, a belief's strength must not be decayed toward zero between sessions. What decays is confidence that the learner still holds it — so the credible interval widens back toward the population prior. A system that decays misconception strength has quietly decided that untested errors repaired themselves.
