What this is

A schedule simulator with a locked effort budget. You cannot buy more practice here. You can only decide which days it happens on, and the page computes what is left in memory a day, a week, a month and a year later.

Why it matters

Every claim that learning "compresses" runs into one wall. Acquisition compresses beautifully. Durability does not — it is built by elapsed time between retrievals, and elapsed time is the one input a tutor cannot manufacture. The good news is that the elapsed time is nearly free.

How to use it

Two questions are worth putting to it. First: how far apart can ten problems be pushed before the return stops rising? Second: if the answer is a fixed number of days, does that number fall out of the arithmetic — or did somebody just quote it at you? Section 3 answers the second one.

1 · A fixed budget, spent on different days

The budget below is a count of practice problems. Splitting them across more sessions does not buy more of them — each session gets a smaller share. Nothing is added. Only the calendar changes.

Your schedule
Sessions are equally spaced. The problems are divided as evenly as the count allows. Retention is measured after the last session.

2 · Where the claim comes from

Rohrer & Taylor (2006), Experiment 1, N = 216. The number of practice problems was held constant at ten. The only manipulation was whether they were massed into one session or split across two sessions a week apart. Both groups were tested at a delay after their final session.

The model gets the one-week row wrong, and it is worth saying so plainly. Rohrer & Taylor found the benefit of splitting was nil at one week — if anything the massed group was slightly ahead, because massing buys short-term fluency that has not yet drained away. The activation model used here is monotone in spacing: it predicts an advantage at every delay. It reproduces the four-week result to about a point and overstates the one-week one. That is a real miss, not a rounding difference, and every number on this page inherits it.
The model, in full — four parameters, no fitting to your inputs

Anderson's base-level activation with Pavlik & Anderson's spacing extension: each practice event lays down its own trace, and the decay rate of a new trace is set by how active the memory already was when it was laid down. Practice something you have just practised and you get a trace that evaporates; practice something you have nearly forgotten and you get one that lasts. That single line is the whole spacing effect.

The four constants were chosen once, by grid search, to land on Rohrer & Taylor's four-week pair. They are not re-fitted when you move a slider — every other number on this page is a prediction from them.

3 · The horizon rule, derived rather than quoted

The survey states that a year-durable memory needs its opportunities separated by roughly 18–36 days. That figure comes from Cepeda et al. (2008), who taught more than 1,350 people a set of facts, reviewed after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and tested up to a year later: the optimal gap, expressed as a share of the test delay, fell from 20–40% at a one-week horizon to 5–10% at a one-year horizon.

None of that is wired into the page. The optimiser below takes your horizon, sweeps the gap in quarter-day steps, and returns the gap that maximises predicted retention. The band it lands in is an output.

Dial a horizon

4 · What this proves, and the conditions on using it

What is actually established here
  • The free lunch is real and it is arithmetic. Ten problems in one sitting and ten problems across four sittings cost the learner the same number of problems. On this page the second is worth roughly an order of magnitude more at a year. The input a tutor cannot buy is the only input that matters, and it is free.
  • The 18–36 day rule is recoverable, not merely citable. Set the horizon to a year and the sweep returns a gap in the low-to-mid twenties to low thirties of days for every session count the budget supports. A four-parameter activation model calibrated on a completely different experiment lands inside a band published from a 1,350-person study it never saw.
  • Adding sessions dominates adding effort. Hold sessions at two and push the budget from 10 problems to 24: one-year retention moves a few points. Hold the budget at 10 and go from two sessions to eight: it moves by a factor of five. The lever is the calendar.
The conditions this runs under
  • A simulated learner is not a learner. Everything above is a forgetting model producing curves. The two effect sizes in the header — g = 0.499 and d = 0.54 — are the measurements; this page is an illustration of the mechanism that produces them, and it inherits the model's miss at one week described in section 2.
  • Spacing is not universal across material. The same interleaving-and-spacing family that carries d = 0.83 in seventh-grade mathematics carries g = −0.39 for paired-associate vocabulary, where blocking wins. A global "space everything" policy helps one and harms the other inside the same session. And a 2020 replication with N = 235 found no distributed-practice effect on mathematical procedures at all, on nearly the material Rohrer & Taylor used. That contradiction is unresolved.
  • The overlearning null is not reproduced. Rohrer & Taylor's Experiment 2 compared three versus nine problems in a single sitting and found the extra six bought nothing at one or four weeks. Feed the same comparison to this model — one session, budget 6 against budget 24 — and it predicts a substantial gain. It is right about the calendar and wrong about the sitting.
  • And the optimiser is the part with the least evidence behind it. This is the boundary worth carrying away. Spacing and retrieval have interventional trials; the scheduling algorithm has almost none. On a 350-million-review benchmark a zero-parameter moving average beats every FSRS version on log loss, a 34-feature logistic regression beats FSRS-6 and -7 on all three metrics, and the FSRS team themselves published a deliberate exploit showing the headline metric is gameable. Those are backtests on logs of what learners already did — predicting a review is not causing a memory. So the win here is spacing-and-retrieval. It is not the optimiser, and a product that sells the optimiser is selling the part with no trials.
