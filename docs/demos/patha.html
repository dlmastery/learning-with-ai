Demo 12 · The mechanisms

The Pāṭha Protocol, and Why It Failed

A three-thousand-year-old error-detection scheme whose deepest trick is that it destroys meaning on purpose, in order to disable the reciter's semantic autocorrect. This project proposed adapting it as a fidelity check on model output, benchmarked it, and falsified it. Both halves are here.

Computed live Shows a null result A falsification of our own proposal
What this is

A three-thousand-year-old error-detection scheme, implemented, and then the benchmark that killed the idea this project built on it.

Why it matters

The Vedic pāṭha recitations permute word order deliberately, which disables semantic autocorrect, the exact failure mode of LLM paraphrase drift. We proposed adapting it as a fidelity check on model output. It was worth trying and it does not work.

How to use it

Corrupt a token and watch krama catch a transposition that plain recitation misses. That part is real and computed. Then read §4, which reports our own benchmark: 87.5%/87.5% and 100%/100%, exactly at chance, losing to plain self-consistency by 18.8 and 43.8 points. This is what publishing a falsification looks like.

What is computed and what is cited, precisely. Sections 1–3 are computed: the permutation schemes are generated from your text by the actual recursions, the corruption is really injected, and the detection and localisation results are really derived by multiset comparison in your browser. Section 4 is a reported measurement: 768 model generations run offline against a deterministic comparator, by a harness in this repository. Those numbers are cited data, exactly like the effect sizes on every other page. No model runs on this page and none is claimed to.

The recitations, generated
Each is a different function of the same token sequence, not a repetition of it. That distinction is the whole design.
What it is, in coding terms, stated without romance. This is not a Hamming code and it is not error-correcting in Shannon's sense — there is no algebraic structure that lets one reciter reconstruct a corrupted symbol. It is (a) a high-rate redundancy scheme with (b) order-dependent consistency checks that give error detection with locality, plus (c) a social decoder: correction happens by quorum among independently trained reciters, and the pāṭha forms tell them where to look. Describing it accurately makes it more impressive, not less. It is a detection-plus-quorum-repair protocol with an explicit anti-semantic-smoothing countermeasure, engineered a millennium and a half before information theory.

2.1 · Why the comparison is by content, not by index

Align two recitations by position and a single deletion makes every subsequent position disagree: the error is detected and its location is completely obscured, because everything after it looks wrong too. Align them by utterance — and in krama, jaṭā and ghana the utterances are short, self-contained, order-bearing n-grams — and the two reciters' utterance sets are identical everywhere except in a neighbourhood of the error.

That is what "detection with locality" means, mechanically. The community does not have to re-derive the text; it has to repair one word, and the pāṭha forms have told it which one. The table above computes the implicated span for each scheme, and you can watch pada-pāṭha — a word-by-word recitation with no order information — fail to detect a transposition that krama catches immediately.

3 · The deep trick: destroying meaning on purpose

The 321 permutation
Read what ghana actually requires you to say. This is not a side effect of the scheme. It is the point of the scheme.

In ordinary oral transmission the single largest source of drift is that reciters unconsciously repair toward sense — they substitute the word that should be there. By forcing recitation of sequences that carry no meaning at all, the pāṭha system disables semantic autocorrect and forces storage of the token sequence itself. There is nothing to smooth toward.

And that is exactly the problem we thought we had. LLM paraphrase drift is semantic autocorrect. A model asked to reproduce a formula, a statute or a dosage table produces the plausible version. So: do not ask the model for the claim twice. Ask it to produce the claim, then to produce order-permuted re-derivations — derive it in a different order, state the inverse, evaluate at boundary values, restate it symbolically and then numerically, reverse each step — and check those against each other. Krama, jaṭā and ghana applied to model output instead of to a verse. The argument was that this is strictly stronger than self-consistency sampling, because the permutations are adversarial to semantic smoothing rather than resampling the same plausible error. It was labelled INFERENCE, flagged as testable, and left for somebody to benchmark. Nobody had. We did.

4 · The benchmark

Reported measurement. 768 generations, two models, one deterministic comparator.

Recall and false-alarm rate, identical in both runs. The protocol flags corrupted claims and correct claims at the same rate, which is another way of saying it is not detecting corruption at all. Self-consistency is a poor detector — 18.8% and 43.8% recall — that never cries wolf, and therefore wins on discrimination by 18.8 and 43.8 points.

The scaling direction is the part to notice. Going from 4B to 8B improved self-consistency's discrimination by +25 points and improved pāṭha's by zero — the larger model simply flagged everything, in both conditions. A protocol whose false-alarm rate rises exactly as fast as its recall does not get better with model scale. It gets louder.
The diagnosis, stated as a general principle

Permutation-based fidelity checking is confounded with probe competence. It detects "the model cannot do algebra" far more reliably than it detects "the claim is corrupted."

The permuted probes are harder tasks, and the model fails them whether or not the claim is corrupted. On gemma3 two of the six probes have negative discrimination. The protocol's signal is swamped by probe difficulty, and the "any disagreement flags" rule aggregates six noisy detectors into one that sits exactly at chance.

And the per-probe rankings do not transfer. inverse is the best probe on gemma3 (+25.0) and nearly the worst on hermes3 (+6.2); numA is the worst on gemma3 (−6.2) and among the best on hermes3 (+31.2). Only forward — the un-permuted probe — is stable, and it is stable precisely because it is the only one both models can execute reliably. Any probe calibration is therefore per-model and per-version, not a property of the protocol.

The original mechanism argument was correct, and that is why it failed. The claim was that structurally different redundancy is not correlated with the original error. It is not. But the permuted probes have their own, independent, much larger error rate, and that independent error rate is what the aggregator ended up measuring.

The rescue, and its honest caveat
We swept all 63 non-empty probe subsets on each model and took the best.
Stated because it is the difference between a finding and an artefact: the subsets were selected post hoc on the same 16 claims. These are oracle upper bounds, not estimates. And read the hermes3 row carefully. Even with oracle probe selection, permutation checking beats self-consistency by 6.2 points on that model, having been selected on the test set to do so. There is no honest reading in which the protocol as specified works.

5 · What publishing a falsification looks like

The pāṭha proposal was ours. It was elegant, it had a three-thousand-year pedigree, the mechanism argument was sound, and it was exactly the kind of idea that gets written up as a technique, cited as a technique, and never tested. It was labelled INFERENCE and explicitly flagged as something for a later section to benchmark. That flag is the only reason this page exists.

What a survey usually doesWhat was done here
Proposes the technique, cites the tradition, labels it "promising" Proposed the technique and labelled it INFERENCE, with a named section responsible for testing it
Moves onBuilt a matched-budget harness with a deterministic comparator so no model could grade itself
Ran 768 generations on two models of different scale
Found it at chance, twice, and published the number rather than the idea
Swept the subsets, found an oracle rescue, and labelled the rescue as an oracle rather than reporting the best number found
Kept the diagnosis, which is more useful than the technique would have been: redundancy that is structurally different is also independently harder

The general lesson is not about Sanskrit. It is that a mechanism argument is a hypothesis, not a result, and the more elegant the mechanism the more urgently it needs a matched-budget baseline. Self-consistency is a dull, obvious, badly-performing baseline. It won.

What this proves / what it does not

What this page proves
  • That the pāṭha permutations are exactly specifiable and trivially implementable. The recursions above are the traditional ones, and they run on any token sequence you type.
  • That detection with locality is real and computable. Compare utterance multisets rather than positions and a single corruption implicates a bounded span, while the same corruption in a flat sequence implicates everything downstream. You can watch the span numbers change as you move the corruption site.
  • That order-bearing redundancy catches what order-free redundancy misses: pada-pāṭha cannot see a transposition; krama sees it immediately. That is a computed result on your text, not an assertion.
  • That the protocol, as specified, does not work as a fidelity check on model output. 768 generations, two models, exactly at chance both times.
What this page does not prove
  • That the historical claim is measured. That the Vedic tradition achieved exceptionally high fidelity over millennia is a historical and philological claim, not an experimental one. Nobody ran the control. The pattern definitions here are standard but the consolidated table is tertiary-sourced, and a Sanskritist should confirm the less common forms.
  • That permutation checking is dead. Two open models at 4B and 8B, sixteen physics formulas, one flagging rule. A frontier model, a different domain, or a probe-weighted aggregator instead of "any disagreement flags" might behave differently. What is refuted is the protocol as specified, which is what was specified.
  • That the oracle subsets mean anything. They were selected on the data they were scored on. They are an upper bound on what probe selection could buy, not an estimate of what it would buy.
  • That this teaches anyone anything. Even the parts that work are error-detection machinery, not instruction. A demo shows a mechanism is buildable and coherent. It is not evidence that it teaches.
