One textbook page, and eight strategies against it

A standard treatment of limits, the kind in every calculus text. We tear it down against the fidelity invariants, then let a diagnosis select which of eight strategies fires. The quantifier checker and the numeric falsifier run for real; the mentor's sentences are scripted.

Checkers computed Dialogue scripted Shows a documented null
What this is

A worked teardown and rebuild of one hard concept. The textbook passage is representative, not quoted from any single book. Everything flagged against it is a named, checkable failure — not a matter of taste.

Why it matters

Students compute limits correctly for two years and then cannot understand uniform convergence. The trace goes back to two sentences nobody flagged. This shows exactly which two, and what a system does instead.

How to use it

Read the passage, then open the flags. Take the probe honestly, because it selects the strategy. Then run the two checkers at the bottom on claims of your own; they are real and they will disagree with you.

1 · The page, as written

Definition. We say that the limit of f(x) as x approaches a is L, written limx→a f(x) = L, if f(x) gets arbitrarily close to L as x gets sufficiently close to a.

Intuitively, the limit describes the value the function is heading towards. We do not care what happens at x = a itself — only what happens nearby. Think of walking along the curve towards a: the height you are approaching is the limit.

Example. limx→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4, since we may cancel the factor (x − 2) to obtain x + 2, which equals 4 at x = 2.

Exercises 4.2  1–18: evaluate each limit.  19–22: state whether the limit exists.

Nothing there is wrong, exactly. It is the treatment most students meet, and most of them pass the exercises. Here is what it does to them.

2 · The teardown

Failure 1 — quantifier strength

“arbitrarily close … sufficiently close” is a natural-language paraphrase of ∀ε ∃δ. It does not encode the order. A reader cannot recover from this sentence whether δ may depend on ε — and that dependency is the definition. It is also the difference between continuity and uniform continuity, which is the second half of a real-analysis course.

This is the invariant a simplification may never falsify, and it is the one mathematics breaks most often. It is also mechanically checkable — see §5 below.

Failure 2 — ontological crossing

“heading towards”, “walking along the curve”, “approaching” place the limit in the process category. A limit is an object — a number that exists.

Chi's test: errors within a category are repairable; errors across categories are robust. Measured instance from chemistry — the Bohr-model hybrid population was unchanged across a full semester of university instruction. A student holding “limit” as a process computes correctly for years and cannot understand uniform convergence, because uniform convergence quantifies over a space of functions — which requires functions to be objects first.

Failure 3 — the example teaches the wrong skill

Cancelling (x − 2) is algebra. It rehearses a manipulation and never touches the definition. A student can complete all eighteen exercises without once using ε or δ — and will, because the exercises are the same skill eighteen times.

Failure 4 — one rung, no entry point

Three explanation rungs beat two (p = 0.032); five did not beat three (p = 0.738). This page has one, pitched at a reader who does not exist. For anyone above it, added exposition is measurably harmful — expertise reversal runs +0.505 for novices and −0.428 for experts.

Failure 5 — no retrieval, no spacing, no feedback, no rule

Retrieval practice is g = 0.50; the page's affordance is re-reading. Spacing is d = 0.54; the page is linear and §4.2 never returns. The answer key says what, never why. And with no measurement there can be no decision rule — the component that, in the randomised trial, was the only thing that moved achievement.

3 · The probe that selects the strategy

Entry is measured, never preferred — stated preference moves at d ≈ 0.48 while knowledge moves zero — a preference effect, unrelated to the teaching-expectancy g = 0.48 in §4. One question, fifteen seconds, and it probes the obstacle rather than the definition.

Why can a limit exist at a point where the function is undefined?
Answer honestly. The system is not scoring you; it is choosing what to do next.

4 · The eight strategies, and which one fires

Each is a technique with a measured effect and a stated failure condition. The diagnosis selects; it does not run all eight. Ones the probe selected are outlined.

What this proves / what it does not

It proves that the two failures flagged in §2 are mechanically checkable, not matters of taste. The quantifier predicate and the numeric falsifier both run here, on your machine, and both will reject a claim you make.

  • It does not prove the rebuilt loop teaches better than the page. No trial has compared them. The claim on this page is about what the artifact is structurally incapable of, which is a different and smaller claim.
  • The passage is representative, not quoted. It is written to match the treatment in common calculus texts; no single book is being criticised.
  • The strategy selection is a rule engine, not a model. Eight strategies, one probe, deterministic mapping. A real system would need the diagnosis to work on free-form student work — which is the thing measured at chance across 223 domains.
  • The numeric checker refutes; it never establishes. Surviving every sample is consistent with the claim and is not a proof of it.
