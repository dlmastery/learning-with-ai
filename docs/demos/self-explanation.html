Demo 18 · Mechanisms

The question that makes you find your own gap

Induced self-explanation pools at g = 0.55 across 69 effect sizes — one of the largest numbers in the corpus for something that costs a sentence. The mechanism is not the prompt. It is that producing an explanation exposes the piece you did not have, to you, before anyone tells you. Below, that exposure is made mechanical.

Coverage and consequences computed Reference decomposition authored A matcher, not comprehension
What this is

A short worked solution with two probe points, and a scorer that checks your explanation against a reference decomposition, the four or five propositions a complete answer has to contain. It reports which ones you hit, which you missed, and what the missing one costs.

Why it matters

Explanation only teaches when the gap becomes visible. "Good effort, but consider the domain" does not do that. Naming the exact proposition you did not produce does, and it is the difference between feedback that flatters and feedback that locates.

How to use it

Write the explanation before opening anything. The reference stays hidden until you submit, because a decomposition you have already read is not a test of anything. Afterwards there is a button that fools the matcher on purpose. Press it, because it shows you precisely what the score is and is not.

1 · The worked solution

Four steps, correct throughout. The probes sit at the two steps where something is happening that a learner can follow arithmetically without understanding at all.

2 · Coverage against the reference decomposition

Not a grade and not a judgement of quality. A count of required propositions produced. The reference is fixed, printed in full, and the same for everyone.

3 · What each miss costs

Here a missing proposition has a computed consequence rather than a rhetorical one. The quadratic is solved in the page, both roots are substituted back into the original radical equation, and the answer you would have reported is derived from which propositions you were missing.

Consequence engine
Runs on the actual equation, not on a stored answer key.
Score your explanations to compute this.

4 · Why coverage and not a judge

An obvious design would hand the explanation to a language model and ask "is this a good explanation?" We do not, and the reason is measured. In this project's own selection benchmark, choosing between candidate outputs by public tests gained +8.14pp, by generated tests +2.70pp, and by LLM judge alone measured −3.20pp and −1.68pp, worse than not selecting at all. A judge is an agreeable instrument. A checklist of propositions is a dumb one, and dumb is what you want in the scoring position.

The matcher, tested against itself
Every honest scorer should ship its own adversarial case.
The decoy is a keyword-stuffed non-explanation. Load it, press score, and watch the coverage bar rise on a string that means nothing.

5 · What this proves / what it does not

What this page demonstrates
  • A reference decomposition is writable and cheap. Seven propositions across two probes, each with an explicit pattern set. Writing them took longer than running them; the scoring itself is timed on your own device in the consequence panel — microseconds — and needs no network, no key and no model.
  • A miss can be turned into a demonstration instead of a correction. If you did not produce "check both candidates in the original", the page does not tell you that you are wrong. It computes the answer set you would have reported and substitutes it back until it fails. The gap is shown, not asserted.
  • Prompting at a step beats prompting at the end. Squaring and checking are the two operations here that a learner can follow arithmetically while understanding nothing, and both probes sit on them. A prompt after step 4 asking "did you understand?" would collect nothing.
What it does not
  • Coverage is not comprehension, and the page proves this against itself. The decoy button loads a string with no propositional content that scores highly. The matcher detects whether the required ideas were mentioned, in any order, with no check on whether they were connected correctly. Anyone shipping this in earnest needs a second, non-linguistic signal. The learner's performance on the next problem is the obvious one.
  • The reference is one person's decomposition. A learner can give a correct explanation by a route the reference does not contain — via the domain restriction x ≥ 1 alone, for instance — and lose coverage for it. That is a false negative built into the method, not a bug in the patterns, and it is why coverage should gate a follow-up question rather than a grade.
  • g = 0.55 is for inducing self-explanation, not for scoring it. Bisra et al. measured the effect of asking learners to explain. Whether machine-scored feedback on the explanation adds anything on top of the asking is a separate question that this page does not answer and the meta-analysis did not test.
  • And the naive combination has a documented failure. Barbieri et al. found self-explanation prompts to be a significant negative moderator of the worked-example effect. Bolting a prompt onto an example is not the way to get this, which is why the probe here interrupts at a specific step with a specific question, rather than appending "explain your reasoning" to a solved problem.
