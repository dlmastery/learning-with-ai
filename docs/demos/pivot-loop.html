Demo 10 · The mechanisms

Two Clocks

A tutor that changes method after three wrong answers is not adapting. It is fitting noise. This page computes the false-pivot rate for the rules people actually ship, derives the number of weeks a trend decision genuinely needs, and separates the loop that is allowed to run in seconds from the loop that has to wait.

Computed live Monte Carlo · exact run-length DP Shows a null result
What this is

Two decision loops, simulated honestly. An exact run-length calculation for “three wrong in a row”, a 4,000-learner Monte Carlo for trend rules, and a closed-form answer for how many weeks a real decision needs.

Why it matters

A tutor that changes method after three wrong answers is not adapting — it is fitting noise, and this page computes exactly how often. Worse: measurement alone is inert. In the randomised trial, both arms revised instruction more often and only the arm told what to change moved achievement.

How to use it

Start with the three-in-a-row rule and read the expected false pivots per term. Then move to the weekly probe loop and drag the number of data points — watch detection and false-alarm trade against each other until the published 7–10 week bound falls out.

Computed means: the false-pivot rates below are Monte-Carlo estimates from simulations run in your browser when you press the button; the three-wrong-answer probability is an exact dynamic-programming result, not a simulation; and the weeks-required figure is a closed-form standard-error calculation you can check by hand. The generative model is stated in full and its parameters are yours to move.

1 · The two clocks

The fast loop — micro-scaffold

seconds to minutes · fires on a single response

Stays inside the current method. Adjusts support, not approach. Cheap to fire, cheap to be wrong about, reversible on the next item.

  • Give the next step of a worked example
  • Reduce element interactivity by splitting the problem
  • Prompt a self-explanation
  • Offer a second aligned instantiation of the same structure
  • Re-state the prerequisite that the error implicates

Evidence floor: computer-based scaffolding is meta-validated at g ≈ 0.46 across 144 studies. This loop is allowed to be twitchy because a wrong micro-scaffold costs one item.

The slow loop — change method

weeks · four data points minimum, trend rules far longer

Abandons the current approach and substitutes a different one. Expensive to fire, expensive to be wrong about, and it discards whatever partial learning the previous method had established.

  • Switch instructional approach for this objective
  • Change the intervention, its dosage, or its group size
  • Move a prerequisite into the foreground
  • Escalate to a human

Evidence floor: the classic four-point rule is the minimum. Trend-line rules are not statistically viable until 7–10 weeks of weekly data, and the growth-claim threshold in the CBM literature is ~14 points over 8–15 weeks.

The failure mode is collapsing the two. Almost every shipped "adaptive" system runs a single loop at the fast clock's tempo and lets it make slow-clock decisions. Three wrong answers, change the topic. Two hesitations, drop the difficulty. Those are slow-loop moves fired on fast-loop evidence, and the sections below quantify exactly how often they fire on a learner who is doing perfectly well.

3 · The weekly probe loop

Now the slow clock. Same question, harder arithmetic.

Simulation parameters
Oral reading fluency in words correct per minute, the canonical curriculum-based measure. One probe per week.
How many weeks does a trend decision actually need?
Closed form, no simulation. The ordinary-least-squares slope over n equally spaced weekly points has standard error σ / √Sxx where Sxx = n(n²−1)/12. Ask for the 95% interval on the slope to be narrower than the goal slope itself — a weak requirement, and it is already expensive.

4 · Measurement alone is inert

The load-bearing finding, and the reason a dashboard is not a product.

Everything above is about when a system is entitled to pivot. This section is about what has to happen when it does — and it is the single most architecturally consequential result in this whole area, because it says that getting the timing right is necessary and not sufficient.

Fuchs, Hamlett & Stecker 1991. 33 teachers randomly assigned to three conditions, 20 weeks, mathematics operations. Verbatim: "Compared to the control group, both CBM groups appeared to revise students' instructional programs more frequently. However, only the CBM-ExS group effected superior student achievement." A companion randomised study in spelling replicated the pattern.

Why this is a table and not a chart. The paper reports the direction and the significance pattern; this page does not have the group means or effect sizes, so plotting bars would mean inventing magnitudes. It is labelled down to a qualitative matrix rather than dressed up.

Read the middle row twice. Frequent measurement plus frequent programme changes, without guidance on what to change, produced no achievement benefit. The benefit came entirely from the component that told the teacher what to do next. An AI tutor that "monitors engagement and adapts" is the middle row. Dashboards, mastery bars, streaks and adaptive difficulty are all measurement. They are not the active ingredient.

What this proves / what it does not

What this page proves
  • That the arithmetic is not close. Under any parameterisation you can reach with the sliders, a three-wrong-in-a-row trigger fires repeatedly, every week, on a learner who is performing exactly as intended. This is not a tuning problem.
  • That the 7–10 week bound is recoverable from first principles. You do not have to take the CBM literature's word for it: the slope standard error over n weekly points is σ/√(n(n²−1)/12), and asking for an interval narrower than the goal slope itself puts you in double digits of weeks at any realistic probe error.
  • That probe equivalence is load-bearing, not hygiene. Toggling from verified-equivalent forms to readability-matched forms visibly moves the weeks-required figure, because form variance enters the slope estimate exactly like random error does.
  • That fast and slow loops are separable in code — different triggers, different evidence thresholds, different action menus, different costs of being wrong.
What this page does not prove
  • That the simulated learner resembles a real one. Independent Bernoulli responses at a fixed accuracy is a convenient fiction; real item responses are correlated, non-stationary, and confounded with fatigue and item order. Every false-pivot number here is a lower bound in the sense that real dependence usually makes runs more common, but I have not measured that and neither should you assume it.
  • That the four-point rule or any trend rule is correct. A systematic review of 102 documents found CBM progress-monitoring decision rules are "based on expert opinion" with "very limited psychometric or empirical support." The rules simulated here are the ones the field uses; that is not the same as the ones the field has validated.
  • That pivoting later is better than pivoting sooner. This page shows that pivoting sooner is mostly responding to noise. It does not show what the optimal policy is, and nobody knows, because the evidence base predates systems that observe hundreds of responses an hour.
  • That waiting seven weeks is right for every learner. The false-pivot arithmetic is exact; the cost of a missed pivot is not modelled here and could dominate it.
