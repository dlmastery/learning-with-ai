Demo 08 · The mechanisms

The Felt-Learning Trap

Satisfaction and learning can move in opposite directions, and the learner cannot feel the difference. This page lets you run the study, computes the statistics for real, and then hands you the falsification test that disqualifies almost every metric a learning product ships with.

Computed live Synthetic participants Shows a null result
What this is

A study you can run. Set how fluent and how effortful a condition feels, and the page computes both satisfaction and delayed retention — with Welch's t and a real p-value, not a cartoon.

Why it matters

This is the central finding of the whole survey. Preference moves at d ≈ 0.48 while knowledge moves zero, and it survives explicit debiasing. Students in the condition that taught them more reported learning less. Every optimisation loop can measure the first axis; almost none measure the second.

How to use it

Make a condition feel maximally fluent and watch satisfaction rise as retention does not. Then run the Null-Learner Test: pick a metric and see whether an agent that teaches nothing can max it out. Engagement, streaks and time-on-task all fail.

Read this before the numbers. Everything on this page is computed — the sampling, the means, the pooled standard deviations, Welch's t, the degrees of freedom and the p-values are all calculated in your browser from data generated on the spot. But the participants are synthetic. The generative model is printed in full below, and its coefficients are choices, not measurements. A simulation can show that a pattern is coherent. It cannot show that the pattern is true. The real anchors — the ones that are measurements — are in the second panel, separately labelled.

The structural problem. Each of those three rows is a case where the signal that is cheap to collect in-session is anti-correlated, or at best uncorrelated, with the outcome anyone actually wants. A system trained on in-session signal does not merely fail to help; it is being actively steered away from the interventions with the best evidence.

3 · The Null-Learner Test

A falsification test you can apply to any metric, in one line.

Simulate an agent that maximises the metric while learning nothing — the cheapest action sequence that satisfies the measurement. If it scores well, the metric is invalid as a learning objective. Below, both agents are actually simulated for 30 days under stated policies, and all five metrics are derived from the resulting event logs. Nothing is hardcoded.

Every metric except the last one fails. DAU, streak, time-on-task and satisfaction are all maximised by an agent whose knowledge state never changes. That is not a criticism of those metrics as health signals — they are fine for detecting an outage. It disqualifies them as objectives. The only metric here that cannot be maxed without learning is a delayed, unannounced, novel-item, unassisted assessment — which is also the most expensive one to collect and the one no consumer product runs.

What this proves / What it does not

What this page proves
  • That a satisfaction–retention divergence is internally coherent: a single plausible generative model, with effort optimal at one level and fluency loading on affect, produces the pattern without any contrivance.
  • That the statistics are real. Welch's t, the Satterthwaite degrees of freedom and the incomplete-beta p-value are computed in this page from the sample you drew.
  • That the Null-Learner Test is mechanically applicable — you can run it against a metric in a few dozen lines, and four of the five metrics here fail it under a simulation whose policies are printed on the page.
What this page does not prove
  • Nothing about real learners. The participants are drawn from a distribution I chose. Changing one coefficient changes every number on the page. Treat the effect sizes here as illustrations of a shape, never as estimates.
  • That effortful instruction is always better. The model has an optimum, and if you drag prior knowledge down while pushing effort up, retention collapses in both arms — which is the expertise-reversal boundary, and it is why "add desirable difficulty" is not a universal instruction.
  • That the three measured anchors generalise beyond their settings. d ≈ 0.48 for preference is a pooled figure across a small number of comparisons; the −17% is a single field experiment in one course.
  • That any of this teaches anyone anything. A demo shows a mechanism is buildable and coherent. It is not evidence that it teaches.
