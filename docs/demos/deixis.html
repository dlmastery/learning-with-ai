Demo 06 · The Village

Pointing At The Thing

Human teachers do not describe position. They point. Here is a tutor that circles, arrows and shades a shared figure while saying “this” — and the same tutor forbidden from pointing, so you can feel what the words cost.

Computed live Drag a vertex Tutoring loop unmeasured
COMPUTED. The figure is not a picture. Every angle, arc, parallel line and pointing gesture is computed from three vertex coordinates, live — drag a vertex and the referents move with it, because they are derived from the object rather than drawn on top of it. That is the actual technical claim behind deixis: a gesture has to resolve to a region of the current scene, not to a location on a canvas. The tutor's sentences are a fixed script, which is labelled below every time it matters.

What that number is and is not. It is a genuine count of the two scripts, and it measures verbosity. It is not a measure of comprehension, effort or learning. The reason to care about the difference is a separate, older result: the spatial-contiguity / split-attention effect, g = 0.63 — text placed next to the thing it describes beats text placed away from it, because the learner does not have to hold a search in working memory while integrating. Deixis is a mechanism for achieving contiguity in a moving, interactive figure. The effect size belongs to contiguity, not to this demo.

Read 49% correctly. It is a genuine advance from a floor of under 1%, on a task that was recently hopeless — and it means that on roughly half the pixels of a typical referent, the model and the ground truth disagree. Buffered IoU at 85% says the model usually points at approximately the right thing. “Approximately the right thing” is a good research result and an ambiguous tutoring signal: a tutor that circles approximately the right angle has, from the learner's side, circled a different angle.

4 · Why the words-only version is not merely wordier

Switch the mode and read turn 4 in both versions. In the deictic version the learner's eye is led to the referent before the claim arrives. In the words-only version the learner must parse a locative description, search the figure, commit to a candidate, and only then receive the claim — and if they picked the wrong candidate, the claim lands on the wrong object and nothing in the interaction detects it. That is the failure the pointing removes. It is also, precisely, the failure a 49%-IoU pointer reintroduces half the time.

What this proves / what it does not

What it proves
  • Deictic reference can be made computational: gestures resolved against a live geometric model, not baked into an image. Drag a vertex and every referent follows.
  • The contrast is felt rather than argued. One toggle, same content, and the words-only version is immediately worse to follow.
  • A shared, manipulable field is buildable with no dependency — SVG, pointer events, and about a hundred lines of trigonometry.
  • The verbosity cost of banning deixis is real and countable.
What it does not prove
  • That pointing teaches better. The comparison — deictic tutor versus the same tutor without gestures, on learning outcomes — has not been run. What exists is the contiguity literature, which is adjacent and not the same claim.
  • That a model could produce these gestures. Here they are hand-authored against known regions. The hard problem is the inverse: taking a natural-language intention and resolving it to the right region, which is the 49%-IoU problem, on synthetic diagrams, in one domain.
  • That deixis survives contact with a messy figure. A triangle has a small, unambiguous set of referents. A photograph of a lab bench, a hand-drawn working, or a student's own sketch does not.
  • That the learner's attention actually went where the circle went. This page has no eye tracking and no comprehension probe. Pointing is an invitation to look; whether it was accepted is exactly what the missing trial would measure.
