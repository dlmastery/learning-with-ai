The Kobayashi Maru Generator

A fresh problem, at a chosen structural difficulty, drawn from a space you can count and address by number. Plus the two findings that decide whether such a generator helps or hurts: difficulty must be semantic, and objection must be unannounced.

Computed live Perceptual difficulty: d = −0.05 Objection wording is templated
What this is

A problem generator whose search space is enumerated rather than claimed. It reports exactly how many distinct items it can address, and measures distinctness rather than asserting it.

Why it matters

An adversary that can generate a fresh, unGoogleable problem at the edge of what you know — forever — is one of the things that was structurally impossible when attention was scarce. But difficulty has to be semantic: difficulty that is merely perceptual measures d = −0.05.

How to use it

Generate a few items and note the index — each is one of a stated finite number. Move the difficulty dial and watch the derivation depth change rather than the surface wording. Read §3 for why an announced devil's advocate backfires.

COMPUTED. The generator, the solver, the step count and the search-space arithmetic all run here. Every item is decoded from an integer index by a deterministic function, so you can type a number and get that exact item back — which is what makes the size claim checkable rather than rhetorical. The one scripted element is the wording of the objection in §5: its content is selected from the item's own structure, but the sentences are templates, and no learner response is simulated anywhere on this page.

3 · The solution the generator already knows

The item is not generated and then hoped about. It is generated from a solution plan, which is why the step count is a fact rather than an estimate.

    4 · Why the surface dial is a trap

    Making a task feel harder is not the same as making it require more. The generation effect — the benefit of producing an answer rather than reading one — is d = 0.40 overall, but it is null for nonwords (d = 0.05) and it reverses for anagrams (d = −0.05). Bertsch et al. (2007), 445 effect sizes.

    ConditiondWhat it tells a generator
    Generation over meaningful semantic structure0.40 The effect is real when the learner has to build meaning.
    Cued recall (the strongest cell)0.55 Production beats recognition (0.46) beats free recall (0.32).
    Nonwords0.05 Nothing to elaborate against. Effort without semantics buys nothing.
    Anagrams — difficulty that is purely perceptual−0.05 It goes backwards. A scrambled cue is harder and worse.
    Generation sets larger than 50 items0.09 Near-vanishing. Unbounded generation is not a licence for unbounded dosage.
    The design rule this forces. An item generator has one honest difficulty axis: how much has to be derived. Ugly numbers, longer sentences, unfamiliar nouns and scrambled clause order all raise perceived effort and none of them raise derivation depth. Bertsch's anagram cell is the measurement that says the difference is not a nicety — a cleverly obfuscated cue is closer to an anagram than to a memory palace, and anagrams score d = −0.05.

    Same objection. Same evidence. One framing sentence of difference. A role-played devil's advocate produces, in the words of the finding, “thinking that was primarily aimed at cognitive bolstering of the initial viewpoint rather than stimulating divergent thought.” Announcing the role tells the learner the objection is not sincerely held, and a challenge you know to be theatre is a challenge you defend against rather than think with. The practical consequence for an AI adversary is uncomfortable: the safety-flavoured disclaimer (“I'll argue the other side for a moment”) is the thing that destroys the mechanism.
    What this page refuses to do. It does not show you a simulated learner bolstering their position, because that would be a claim dressed as a demonstration. The bolstering result is a citation, not an output of this page. What is computed here is the objection selection — which structural trap in this item to press on — and that much you can verify by changing the difficulty dial and watching the objection follow the flags.

    What this proves / what it does not

    What it proves
    • Item generation can be counted and addressed. A generator whose space you can state, index into, and sample for collisions is a different object from a model asked to “make me a hard problem”.
    • Difficulty can be defined structurally and computed — derivation steps counted from a solution plan the generator holds — rather than asserted on a 1–5 scale.
    • The surface/structure distinction is demonstrable in one screen: three buttons that change the text substantially and the step count not at all.
    • Objection content can be derived from item structure rather than produced generically, which is what makes an adversary specific enough to be worth arguing with.
    What it does not prove
    • That novel items teach better than reused ones. No trial here. Unbounded generation solves a leakage and reuse problem, not a learning problem, and Bertsch's >50-item cell (d = 0.09) is a warning against assuming more items is more learning.
    • That derivation-step count is the right operationalisation of semantic difficulty. It is a structural measure and a defensible one. It is not validated against learning.
    • That one template space generalises. This is rate-and-capacity word problems. The space is large because the template is parameterised, not because the generator is creative — and a domain without a closed-form solver does not get this treatment.
    • That generation is “unbounded”. It is not, and the page says the number out loud for that reason: — combinations from one template, of which — were distinct in the sample measured at the top of this page. Unboundedness would require a generator that invents new structure, and this one only fills in slots.
    • That an unannounced AI objection reproduces the human devil's-advocate finding. The bolstering result is about humans role-playing for other humans. Whether a learner treats an unannounced AI challenge as sincere is an open question, and arguably a harder one, because the learner knows throughout that they are talking to a machine.
