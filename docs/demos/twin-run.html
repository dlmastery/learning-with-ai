Demo 15 · The falsifier

Your wrong rule, executed until the world disagrees

Pick a belief. It is compiled into a physical law and integrated beside the canonical one from a provably identical start. Nothing here asserts you are wrong — the two worlds simply stop agreeing, and the tick where that happens is found by search.

Fully computed Includes a gate that fails a correct simulation
What this is

A working twin-run falsifier. Two integrations, one shared initial state, a divergence detector, and the verification gates that decide whether a generated simulation is allowed to render at all.

Why it matters

Telling a learner they are wrong requires them to believe you. Running their rule until it visibly fails does not. And the naive implementation — prompting a model to hold the misconception — is measured dead: across seven models the belief is abandoned under any corrective signal.

How to use it

Choose a belief, then press run. Read the divergence tick and the one-line diff. Then open the gates and find G6 — it fails a simulation that is physically correct, which is the point.

2 · The gates that decide whether it may render

A generated simulation is a declarative specification that a verifier checks before a deterministic engine executes it. Six gates run in milliseconds. Five check correctness. One checks something nobody else checks.

Verification
Press Check the gates above.
G6 is the interesting one. It fails a simulation that is physically correct — a free-fall world carrying a mass slider that changes nothing, because in a vacuum it cannot. Every existing verification approach passes that world, because they check whether the physics is right and none checks whether the control teaches anything. A slider that does nothing is a lie about what matters, and it is the most common defect in educational simulations.

3 · What this proves / what it does not

  • It proves the falsifier is cheap and real. Both integrations, the divergence search and all six gates run here, in your browser, in milliseconds, with no model in the loop.
  • It does not prove the learner changes their mind. This is the honest limit and it has a literature: there are seven documented responses to data that contradicts a belief, and only one of them is theory change. Ignoring it, rejecting it, excluding it from the theory's scope, and reinterpreting it are all more common.
  • And the bar is higher than it looks. Refutation text — simply stating the misconception aloud in prose — measures g = 0.41 across 44 comparisons and costs nothing. A simulation has to beat free.
  • Three of the seven escape routes are closed structurally here: the identical-start guarantee removes "your setup was different", the predict-before-observe step removes post-hoc reinterpretation, and asking the learner to commit to the rule first removes "that was not what I meant".
