Demo 17 · Mechanisms

Watch the scaffold come off

Worked examples measure g = 0.48 across 181 effect sizes, and then reverse on the learner who no longer needs them. So the interesting object is not the example. It is the removal schedule, and here it is, running on a real four-step calculation, with the rule that decides each removal printed on the page and evaluated in front of you.

Problems, marking and fade decisions all computed Six problem templates, authored Fading did not moderate scaffolding in 144 studies
What this is

A completion-problem sequence over combustion stoichiometry. Stage 0 hands you the whole solution; stage 4 hands you nothing but the question. In between, blanks appear from the bottom up. Every number is generated from atomic masses and a balanced equation computed at load. Nothing is stored.

Why it matters

A worked example is a gift to someone who cannot yet do the steps and an obstacle to someone who can. The scaffold therefore has to come off, and the only defensible trigger is evidence of unaided competence, which means the system needs something to measure before it is allowed to withdraw anything.

How to use it

Work a problem badly on purpose and the ladder moves backwards; work one cleanly and it moves forward. Either way the rule trace underneath prints the exact inputs it branched on. The fade threshold is a slider, so the rule is not just visible but arguable.

What this replay cannot tell you. It compares schedules, not outcomes. Your accuracy at a stage you never attempted is unobserved, and no amount of arithmetic recovers it. That is not a shortcoming of this page but the reason the fading literature is thin: measuring a fade schedule properly requires randomising learners to schedules and testing them later, and almost nobody has.

5 · What this proves / what it does not

What is demonstrated here
  • The completion-problem gradient is real and mechanical. One problem generator produces five different instructional objects — study, one blank, two, three, all four — from the same balanced equation. Building the gradient costs nothing; the reason it is rare in software is not difficulty.
  • The fade trigger can be made inspectable. The rule is nine lines, its inputs are printed, its branch is highlighted, and its threshold is a control you can move. Compare that to a system that says only "we adapt to you".
  • Withdrawal needs unaided evidence, and stage 0 has none. The study stage carries a comprehension check, and the rule explicitly refuses to count it as competence; the check only prevents click-through. When there is no unaided evidence, any advancement is an assumption, and the page marks it as one.
Which parts are evidenced, and which are design
  • Evidenced: that worked examples beat unguided problem solving for learners without the schema — g = 0.48, 181 effect sizes — and that the advantage reverses with expertise. That much licenses stage 0 for a novice and stage 4 for someone fluent.
  • Not evidenced, and this is the load-bearing caveat: that the path between them matters. Belland, Walker, Kim & Lefler's meta-analysis of 144 experimental studies and 333 outcomes found no moderation by "presence or absence of scaffolding change, and the logic by which scaffolding change is implemented". The earlier pilot found something worse for the tidy story: no fading outperformed fixed fading, with fading or its absence accounting for 30% of variability. Adaptive fading is a design claim resting on the expertise-reversal mechanism, not a measured effect.
  • Also not evidenced: that bolting a prompt onto an example improves it. Barbieri et al. found self-explanation prompts a significant negative moderator of the worked-example effect. This page deliberately does not add one.
  • The honest position: fade on unaided evidence or do not fade. A fixed schedule is the one option the evidence actively discourages, and it is the one almost every curriculum ships.
