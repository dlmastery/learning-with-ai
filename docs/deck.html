The insight

Pedagogy has no pytest.

chance

Across 223 real tutoring domains, no model beat chance at labelling an incorrect student action.

The most basic thing a tutor does — look at what a learner did and say what is wrong with it — is unverifiable by the systems being sold to do it.

The value of an agentic loop is bounded by the value of the external check it closes on. Every capability in this category is downstream of one instrument nobody has built.

The wedge

In exam prep, somebody else administers the truth.

SAT · PSAT · JEE · NEET · GATE · EAMCET. Proctored. Novel items each cycle. Months after study. Scored and reported — because the candidate wants it reported.

The delayed, unassisted, novel-item outcome is the thing that costs every competitor a research programme. Here it is the customer's own goal, on a known date.

Three more things line up: the syllabus is closed so prior knowledge is measurable in 15–40 seconds; the counterfactual is bad, which is where returns are largest; and the exam supplies the motivation, so we need no points economy — the mechanism that measurably undermines intrinsic motivation.

Why humans in the loop

The tutor is not the cost line. The tutor is the labelling function.

What the trial measured

74.4% of 3,617 AI drafts accepted unedited

44.3% of tutor edits were slowing the model's questioning down

What that describes

The human was not writing the content.

The human was acting as the verifier — in a domain where no automated check exists.

Every accept, edit and rejection is a labelled judgement about whether a pedagogical action was correct. The corpus is a by-product of trading.

Stated precisely: the acceptance rate is measured; the authors say their design "precludes a rigorous measurement of throughput or efficiency." We treat 74.4% as a signal stream, never as a productivity claim.

The economics, corrected

"Our costs fall as models get cheaper" is worth 0.43%.

0.43%

Inference as a share of the delivered cost of a human-supervised session. Two independent measurements converge.

Falling token prices are economically irrelevant to this P&L. The entire margin question is the leverage ratio — learner-hours supervised per paid tutor-hour — and that is the number we will report monthly from month one.

What would disqualify us

The three questions. Ask them of us, and of everyone else.

  1. Show me the delayed, unassisted, novel-item result — with n, and who held the item bank. Disqualifying: the only evidence is in-product mastery or engagement. One deployment moved the exit ticket +4pp and was null on the state test.
  2. Have you ever run an arm against plain ChatGPT? Disqualifying: never tried, or tried and buried it. If the pedagogy has never beaten the free substitute customers already have, the moat is a prompt.
  3. Show me learner-hours delivered ÷ paid tutor-hours, monthly, for 24 months. Disqualifying: cannot produce it, or it is flat while headcount grows — which means the AI is decorative and you are underwriting a staffing business at a software multiple.
Risks we are not hiding

Three things that could kill this.

1 · A frontier release solves error diagnosis

If a general model crosses from chance to competent at step-level diagnosis, the verifier becomes an API call. Largest risk, and not remote.

2 · The only audited comparable went the wrong way

A listed tutoring company rebuilt on "entirely new, AI-native codebases." Gross margin 67.5% → 58.0%; expert costs up $5.2M on revenue down $11.2M. Its 10-K: "There can be no assurance that our investments in AI will be beneficial to our business."

3 · One mentor across every book is a named, unstudied risk

Thirty teachers make thirty different mistakes and the system averages them out. One mentor's errors are systematic and identical for every reader at once. We named this correlated pedagogical error; there is no monitoring for it and no name for it in the literature. The mitigation is the same instrument as the moat.
