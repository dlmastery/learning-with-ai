The mission

Everyone becomes a polymath.

Anything you want to understand, understood — at whatever depth you choose, starting this afternoon.

For a nine-year-old, a surgeon, and a forty-year-old who always wondered how bridges stay up. The end of having to pick which curiosities you can afford.

Why that is not a fantasy

Polymathy was never about intelligence. It was about who could afford to be a beginner.

1.14×

how much people differ in learning rate — 25th to 75th percentile, across 1.3 million observations

3.6×

how much they differ in where they start

Scientists work in three or four fields in an entire career. Not because the fifth was too hard — because the orientation cost of entering it was months of being lost, alone, with nobody to ask.

That cost is the entire barrier. It is made of search, of not knowing the real question, of not knowing which results matter. All of it is attention. None of it is talent.

What we are building

Not a tutor. A university that fits in a pocket and knows only you.

The faculty

A mentor who withholds, asks, and waits

A diagnostician watching for the belief behind the wrong answer

A simulator that builds the world the idea lives in — and runs your wrong rule until it breaks

An adversary that objects, genuinely and unannounced

A student you teach, who stays wrong until you actually fix it

An archivist that remembers everything, for years, and belongs to you

What that has never existed as

Every one of these is a person a rich family hires.

None of them has ever been available to one child at once, for a decade, in every subject, at three in the morning.

Three to five of them are active in any hour. The others are waiting.

Who it is for

An eleven-year-old who can explain photosynthesis and cannot pass a worksheet about it.

She reads. But decoding costs her so much that by the end of a sentence the beginning is gone. She is not slow. She is running a working memory that spends everything on the part everyone else automated years ago.

She is one child in seven, and she is the reason the system has to be built properly rather than impressively. A tutor that requires you to notice you are lost, ask a good question, and stay with it — that tutor works for the children who were already fine.

Build it so it works for her and it works for everyone. Build it for everyone and it will not work for her. This is not a segment. It is the specification.

What a day looks like

You spend an hour. It is an hour of learning, not an hour of school.

Nine nominal hours a week sit inside 112 waking ones — and inside those nine, the same nominal day yields four minutes of real learning, or fifty-two, depending on things nobody can see.

What disappears

Waiting for the class to reach you

Re-reading, which is what most study is and is close to useless

Being stuck on a prerequisite nobody diagnosed

Finding the right explanation. Finding anything

What is left

10–40× on the calendar. 3–5× on effort.

A week's understanding in an hour.

A year's retention in six hours spread over two months — because memory needs elapsed time and nothing shortens it, but it costs almost nothing extra.

Why now

Two things became true, and one thing did not.

True now

Presence is real-time. 25 frames a second at ~200 ms — inside the gap between two people speaking.

Knowing what someone knows got cheap. 15–40 seconds for an estimate that used to take a test.

Still not true

Nothing can tell when a learner is stuck. Across 223 real tutoring domains, no model beats chance at saying what is wrong with a student's answer.

Everyone is waiting for that to be fixed. We do not have to.

The idea that makes it work

Stop asking a machine to judge. Ask which belief predicted the answer.

Everyone asks a model "is this wrong, and why?" — an open question over an unbounded space, which is exactly what measures at chance.

We enumerate what a learner might believe. Then the question is which of these predicted what I just saw — and that is a lookup, not a judgement.

What it buys

Chance-level judgement becomes a table lookup

No language model on the live path — so 200 ms is real, not aspirational

Your wrong rule can be executed until the world contradicts it. Reality corrects you, not our authority

Why it compounds

Every learner who is wrong in a new way makes the library better.

A better base model makes this more valuable, not less — which is the only kind of advantage worth having right now.

Why a decade of money produced so little

Because the thing a learner can feel runs opposite to the thing that happened.

Preference moves at d ≈ 0.48 while knowledge does not move at all. Students in the condition that taught them more reported learning less.

So every metric a product can cheaply optimise — satisfaction, engagement, streaks, time-on-app — is the wrong one, and chasing it feels like winning the entire time. That is the trap, it is well measured, and almost every company in this category is inside it.

And the deepest version: help only arrives when a learner recognises they need it. The best-instrumented tutor ever built published its own null and found exactly this. A tutor you must summon reaches the students who need it least.

Where it starts

Where the world already grades the answer.

SAT · JEE · NEET · GATE. Proctored, months later, on problems nobody has seen — and the result comes back because the student wants it to.

Every competitor has to fund a research programme to find out whether their product works. Here, the honest measurement is the customer's own goal. Closed syllabus, high stakes, and hundreds of millions of learners whose alternative is a room with four hundred other people in it.

It is where a system like this gets good. It is not where it stops.

What it costs to be wrong

Three things could kill this, and we would rather say them now.

1 · The models get good at diagnosis

Our structure becomes an API call. The largest risk, and not remote.

2 · One mind, ten million children

Thirty teachers make thirty different mistakes and they cancel out. One system's mistakes are identical for everyone at once. There is no name for this in the literature. We gave it one, and the mitigation is the same instrument as the moat.

3 · It works, and it widens the gap

Untargeted delivery reliably helps the already-strong more. Across eight targeted interventions, none widened gaps — so this is avoidable, not inevitable. We report gap change as a primary outcome, not a footnote. If it widens gaps it has failed, whatever the average says.

The ask

Nobody's thumb, ever again.

Ekalavya was refused a teacher, taught himself, became the better archer — and they took his thumb for it. That is the whole history of education in one story: not a shortage of talent, a shortage of access, and then a system defending itself when someone routed around it.

The tutor was always the intervention that worked. It was never the one that could be given to everyone. That constraint is gone and nothing has been rebuilt around its absence.
