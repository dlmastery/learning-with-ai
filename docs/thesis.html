The core competency

Pedagogy has no pytest

Every agentic capability in education is bounded by one missing instrument. Nobody has built it. And a human-in-the-loop tutoring model is the only structure that builds it as a by-product of operating.

Everything on this page is drawn from the survey and traces to a labelled source. Numbers that could not be verified are not here.

1. The rule

An agent differs from a chatbot in exactly four ways — it can sample many times, execute and see what happened, persist state across a boundary, and work in your absence. Every one of those is a multiplier on something else. None produces value alone.

The value of an agentic loop is bounded by the value of the external check it closes on.

Sampling without a selector is noise. Execution without a test is output. Persistence without a schema is a transcript. Absence without a verifier is unsupervised drift.

2. The rule predicts the whole landscape

Look at where agents work and where they collapse. The axis is not task difficulty.

SWE-bench Verified79.2%strong check — pytest
Terminal-Bench 2.183.8%strong check — it runs or it doesn't
PaperBench21.0%no check
SciCode4.6%weak check — hand-written tests

A twenty-fold spread on how good the check is. Coding agents work because pytest exists. SciCode is not check-free — it has hand-written tests — and still lands at 4.6%, which is why the rule is a bound and not an equality: a weak check caps you low; a strong one only stops you being capped.

Now place tutoring on that scale. It sits in the bottom half, and the reason is measured rather than argued:

Across 223 real tutoring domains, no model beat chance at labelling an incorrect student action.

Not "performed poorly." Chance. The most basic operation a tutor performs — look at what a learner did and say what is wrong with it — is currently unverifiable by the systems being sold to do it.

3. What that means commercially

1

Every AI-tutoring capability is downstream of the verifier. Adaptive pivoting needs to know the learner is wrong and how. Generate-and-select needs a selector — and selection by test measures +8.14pp against −3.20pp for selection by LLM judge, an eleven-point spread in the wrong direction for the judge. A teachable agent needs its tutee's downstream accuracy as ground truth. None of it works without the instrument.

2

The field is currently optimising against two instruments that do not work. The leading agentic-education systems are tuned against LLM-simulated students and scored by LLM-as-judge. Simulated students show near-zero misconception faithfulness across seven models — they do not hold the belief they are role-playing. And the judge is worse than not selecting at all.

3

Nobody can currently prove efficacy, which means nobody can win procurement on it. A census across 20 education-AI subfields: 2,907 arXiv papers, at most 1.79% carrying any learning-outcome marker, eight subfields at exactly zero. ERIC holds 1,565 records on ChatGPT in education and seven randomised trials, four of them second-language learning. The first company that can demonstrate a delayed unassisted outcome is not competing on features.

4. Why humans-in-the-loop is the wedge, not the cost line

The usual framing treats human tutors as the expensive part to be automated away. The evidence says the opposite, and it comes from the only trial that measured a human-supervised AI tutor at scale.

Tutors accepted 74.4% of 3,617 AI-generated drafts unedited. That number is usually quoted as an efficiency win. The interesting number is the other one:

44.3% of tutor edits were slowing the AI's questioning down before it exasperated the student.

Read what that describes. The human was not writing the content. The human was acting as the verifier — catching the cases where the model's pedagogical judgement was wrong, in a domain where no automated check exists.

Which yields the structural argument:

→

A human-in-the-loop tutoring operation is a labelled-data engine for exactly the instrument the category is missing. Every accept, every edit, and every rejection is a labelled judgement about whether a pedagogical action was correct — the training signal for a step-level verifier, generated as a by-product of delivering the service.

The humans are not the cost to be removed. They are the reason the asset compounds. A pure-AI competitor has no mechanism to generate this data. A pure-human competitor generates it and does not capture it.

5. The Cursor pattern, and where it breaks in education

The closest existing template is what happened in coding. It is worth stating precisely, because the analogy holds in three places and fails in one — and the one where it fails is the whole strategy.

What the pattern actually is

1

Wrap the frontier model to get distribution. No proprietary model at the start. The product is the surface, not the intelligence.

2

Instrument the inner loop, not the chat. The defensible signal was never the conversation. It was accept · reject · edit on a suggestion — fired hundreds of times an hour, by every user, for free.

3

Train the narrow model the labs are not optimising for. Low-latency completion is a different task from reasoning, and frontier labs deprioritise it. Owning a task the labs do not want is the only kind of model advantage that survives their next release.

4

A better base model becomes an input, not a threat. This is the test of a real moat: when the frontier improves, does your advantage widen or evaporate?

OBSERVED — the pattern is inferred from public product behaviour, not from disclosed internals. Treat the mechanism as the claim; the company is the illustration.

Three parts transfer directly

Tutoring has the same inner loop and nobody is capturing it. In the one trial that measured a human-supervised AI tutor at scale, tutors accepted 74.4% of 3,617 drafts unedited — and 44.3% of their edits were slowing the model's questioning down. That is accept · reject · edit, on a pedagogical decision, generated by the act of doing the job.

The narrow task is equally clear. Not lesson planning, not curriculum, not the chat: step-level error diagnosis and next-move selection. High frequency, low latency, narrow, and — at chance across 223 domains — nowhere near solved by the labs.

The part that does not transfer, and it decides everything

In coding, the accept signal is validated within seconds. In tutoring, you find out in six weeks.

A developer accepts a completion and the code compiles, the tests pass or fail, the thing runs. The label is cheap to collect and immediately grounded — which is the same fact as pytest existing, arriving from the other direction.

A tutor accepts a draft. Was it the right pedagogical move? The honest answer is that you cannot know from the acceptance. Accepting is a judgement about plausibility, and plausibility is exactly the signal this survey shows to be unreliable — felt learning and real learning move in opposite directions, preference shifting at d ≈ 0.48 while knowledge does not move at all.

So an education company copying the pattern naively builds a very large corpus of what looked right to a tutor at the time. That is a fluency model. It is not a verifier, and it will confidently reproduce the field's existing errors at scale.

Which yields the actual strategy

→

You have to close the loop to outcome, and it has to be inside the product. The accept/reject stream supplies volume. A delayed, unassisted, novel-item check supplies truth. Neither is sufficient; the pairing is the asset.

That is a product decision, not a research decision — it means shipping the delayed test as a routine feature rather than running it once for a white paper. It costs conversion. It is the only thing that converts a plausibility corpus into a verifier.

And it is why this is hard enough to be worth doing. Everyone in the category already measures satisfaction and completion. Almost nobody measures anything after the tool is taken away — ERIC returns zero records for "retention test" AND "ChatGPT". A competitor cannot casually add this later: the label is only worth having if it was attached to the decision at the time it was made.

Two ways the analogy could mislead you

Volume is an order of magnitude lower. A completion fires hundreds of times an hour; a pedagogical decision fires perhaps twenty to sixty. The corpus accumulates roughly 10× slower per user-hour, which changes the scale at which the model becomes good and therefore the funding profile.

And the data-moat thesis is contested even in coding. A serious reading is that distribution and product craft did the work and the model followed. Assume that is at least partly true here — the verifier is the durable asset, and it will not save a product nobody uses.

7. The recursive loop — and why it is the same asset again

The deep-tech version of this is a system that improves its own instruction from its own outcome data. It is the most valuable unbuilt thing in the field, and the reason nobody has it is not ambition.

Self-improvement optimisers have spent three years maximising benchmark accuracy. Instructional-policy research closed the loop on real human retention with 2014 machinery. The two literatures have never met.

Six arXiv queries and ten ERIC queries confirm it: zero optimiser-in-the-loop trials on human learners. Meanwhile a model-based personalised scheduler, deployed for a full semester in a middle-school course, produced +16.5% retention — with the machinery of 2014.

The engineering problem, stated exactly

An optimiser needs thousands of fitness evaluations. A delayed unassisted retention test yields one evaluation per learner per six weeks, with enormous variance. That gap — not compute, not model quality — is why the loop does not exist.

Which forces a three-tier fitness function, and the middle tier is the thing this whole page is about:

TierSignalLatencyVolumeTrustworthy?
FastTutor accept / reject / editSecondsEnormousNo — it measures plausibility
SurrogateThe verifierSecondsEnormousOnly if calibrated against truth
TruthDelayed, unassisted, novel itemWeeksSparseYes

The recursive loop is not a separate play. It is what the verifier unlocks. The verifier is the surrogate fitness function — the only thing that can stand in for a six-week outcome at the frequency an optimiser needs. Build it and the loop follows; skip it and you are optimising plausibility at scale, which is the failure mode already documented in the field's leading systems.

8. Auto-research: one half is done, the other is not automatable

Reading is solved well enough to rely on. PaperQA2 matches or exceeds subject-matter experts on literature synthesis, with 70% of flagged contradictions validated. This survey is an existence proof of the same thing at scale: 35 research reports, ~2,300 sources, and 41 published corrections — 20 of them found by adversarial review rather than by the authors.

Generating new evidence is not. It needs learners, consent, and elapsed time. And there is a constraint people underestimate: across 16 studies and 5,873 participants, people object to being experimented on even when they find either arm individually acceptable — and the effect is undiminished among professionals.

So auto-research accelerates the reading and the design of experiments, and cannot accelerate their running. The realistic claim is a system that continuously re-derives what is known, flags where the corpus contradicts itself, and proposes the next experiment — which is worth a great deal and is not the same as a self-improving tutor.

9. Video-in: there is a legal wall, and it is in the wrong place

Prohibited, not merely regulated

EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) bans emotion inference in education. Not high-risk — prohibited, and in force since February 2025. Reading a student's face to infer frustration, confusion or engagement is the one thing on this list that is illegal rather than difficult.

Article 3(39) restricts the ban to biometric-derived inference, which leaves clickstream-based affect detection — the dominant technique in the research literature — in a grey zone. That is the highest-value unresolved legal question in the field.

And it would be the wrong thing to build anyway. Pedagogical avatars measure g ≈ 0.19, and three 2024 field experiments improved affect at d = .85–1.01 while learning did not move at all. Emotion-responsive tutoring optimises the felt axis — the one this survey shows runs opposite to the real one.

What video-in is genuinely for

1

Watching the work, not the face. Answer-position recognition on student work reaches 98.4%. One mandatory caveat: vision-language models silently repair student errors while transcribing them — they return the corrected work, because that is what training rewards. The mistakes are the entire reason for looking. Transcription must be constrained to a verbatim, error-preserving task and checked against exactly that.

2

Watching gesture — and this is the unexploited one. In a three-arm trial teaching the same strategy as physical action, concrete gesture and abstract gesture, only gesture transferred. Handling the objects produced learning that stayed stuck to the objects.

Gesture is representational compression — it has already thrown away the specific instance and kept the structure. So a camera watching a learner's hands is watching the leading indicator of transfer, weeks before a test could show it. It is legal, it is grounded in the one result that reverses the manipulatives intuition, and nobody is building it.

Video-out

The substrate is real: 640×368 at 25 FPS, ~200 ms model-side latency — inside the human conversational turn-gap — with a persistent world and a separate event stream. The limit is equally real: generated worlds get physics wrong, at 39.6% adherence in the best case and 22% on the hard subset. Keep the world generative and the physics symbolic.

10. Gamification: the moderators point away from what everyone ships

Gamification's cognitive effect is real and survives a high-rigour subsplit. The claim vendors actually make — it keeps people coming back — rests on the weakest and least stable cell in the table: behavioural g = .25, with the confidence interval's lower bound at .04.

And the moderator analysis points somewhere else entirely:

The significant moderators are game fiction and social interaction — with combining competition and collaboration particularly effective. Nothing supports points, badges or leaderboards as the active ingredient.

Three further results that should govern any roadmap here:

a

Novelty decay is measured, not folklore. Perceived benefit declines with time on the service.

b

Even theory-driven design dips. A deliberately need-supporting implementation, measured four times across 15 weeks, produced a curvilinear autonomous motivation curve — an initial downward trend that only later recovered. The companion paper is titled "Unravelling the ambivalent motivational power of gamification."

c

The undermining effect is specific. Across 128 experiments: tangible, expected, performance-contingent rewards significantly undermine intrinsic motivation. That is the exact description of an XP bar.

So the buildable version is fiction and genuine social structure, never a points economy — and no engagement metric may serve as an objective, because streaks, XP, DAU and time-on-app all pass the Null-Learner Test, meaning an agent that teaches nothing can max them out.

11. What makes it hard, which is the point

If it were easy it would not be a moat. Three things make it hard, and all three are surmountable by an operator rather than by a lab:

a

The labels must come from real student work, in the wild. Simulated students have near-zero misconception faithfulness. You cannot synthesise this corpus.

b

Judgement of a pedagogical action is not a single ground truth. It needs the tutor's decision, the learner's subsequent behaviour, and a delayed outcome — which means you need the delivery relationship, not just the transcripts.

c

Decades of labelled intelligent-tutoring logs already exist and are public. The cold-start is smaller than it looks. The public testbed exists. Nobody has done it.

12. The three questions to ask any competitor

1

"Show me the delayed, unassisted, novel-item outcome." Not the practice score, not the same items. Weeks later, device closed. ERIC returns zero records for "retention test" AND "ChatGPT". Without it, nothing was measured about learning.

2

"What was your control condition?" If it was nothing, the result is uninformative — against nothing, almost everything works. The bar is plain ChatGPT, because that is what a learner has for free.

3

"What happened to the gap, not the mean?" Untargeted delivery reliably loads on already-strong learners. A healthy mean concealing a widened gap has made things worse.

13. What would kill this thesis

Stated in advance, so it can be checked

A frontier release that labels student errors reliably. If a general model crosses from chance to competent at step-level error diagnosis, the verifier stops being a proprietary asset and becomes an API call. This is the single largest risk and it is not remote.

The counterfactual result generalising. In a controlled trial, two designed pedagogical interventions produced no significant advantage over plain ChatGPT. One reading of the whole corpus is that returns on pedagogical design scale with how bad the counterfactual was — largest where instruction is scarce, near zero in a well-resourced classroom. If that holds, the addressable market is not where the money is.

And the honest prior on the adjacent claim: four separate attempts to reduce special-education administrative burden — computerisation, delegation, deregulation, and a statutory waiver authority — were measured, and all four returned zero. The AI claim is the fifth attempt, and its entire measured base is one RCT of 22 novice teachers.

14. The one-line version

Everyone is building the tutor. The bottleneck is the examiner — and a human-in-the-loop operation is the only structure that produces one while it trades.
