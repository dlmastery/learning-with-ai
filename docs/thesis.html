The core competency

The instrument that unlocks every other capability

Everything an agentic tutor can do — pivoting, generating, teaching back, intervening before you ask — is waiting on one component. It is buildable now, it compounds with use, and every frontier release makes it more valuable. This is the case for building it.

Everything on this page is drawn from the survey and traces to a labelled source. Numbers that could not be verified are not here.

1. The artifact is structurally incapable of what works

This is not a complaint about badly written textbooks. Most are carefully made. The indictment is narrower and worse: a printed book cannot do the things that are measured to work, and every one of those things is now cheap.

What is measured to workEffectWhat a book can do about it
Retrieval practice, testing yourselfg = 0.50Its primary affordance is re-reading, which students report using most and which "does not consistently boost performance"
Spacing, returning to materiald = 0.54A book is linear. Chapter 3 never comes back

Explanation at more than one altitude3 > 2, p = .032A book has exactly one rung

And the failure is silent. The book cannot tell whether it worked, so nothing in the system knows it failed. A learner who has read the chapter and understood nothing produces the same observable as one who mastered it.

The number that is the deal-breaker

All of this is usually defended by saying the book was never meant to work alone, since a teacher supplies what it cannot. That defence is correct, and it has been quantified:

g = 1.426 with teacher support. g = 0.077 without.

A meta-analysis of 19 studies reports an overall effect of 0.683 that decomposes almost entirely onto the human. Take the teacher out and the effect is gone, not smaller.

That is the problem in one line. The material is a delivery vehicle for a teacher's judgement, and there are not enough teachers. Every learner without a good one is holding an artifact operating at 0.077.

Two failures that survive the teacher

1

Fluent exposition feels like learning and is not. The clearest, best-designed chapter produces the strongest sense of understanding. In the controlled comparison, students in the condition that taught them more reported learning less. A well-written book is optimised for the axis that runs opposite to the real one.

2

A simplification in the wrong ontological category is permanent. The Bohr-model hybrid population was unchanged across a full semester of university chemistry. When a book puts a concept in the wrong category to make it approachable, it creates a misconception that a semester of instruction does not shift, and nobody discovers it, because nothing is measuring.

And the end-of-chapter questions were never calibrated

An item bank was valuable because it held calibrated items, with known difficulty, known discrimination, and distractors mapped to real student errors. Practically no textbook's exercises have ever been validated against response data. They are plausible questions written by an expert, which is the thing the assessment literature says is insufficient: experts classify their own items' quality at below chance.

The book is well made and structurally inert. It is fixed, written at one altitude, for a reader who does not exist, with no memory, no schedule, no diagnosis and no feedback, and it has been carrying the entire load in every setting where the teacher is absent, overloaded, or teaching thirty other children.

2. The rule

Four things separate an agent from a chatbot. It can sample repeatedly, run something and observe the result, hold state across a session boundary, and act while you are elsewhere. Each of the four multiplies something else, and none produces value alone.

An agentic loop is worth no more than the external check it closes on.

Sampling without a selector is noise. Execution without a test is output. Persistence without a schema is a transcript. Absence without a verifier is unsupervised drift.

3. The rule predicts the spread

Look at where agents work and where they collapse. The axis is not task difficulty.

SWE-bench Verified79.2%strong check: pytest
Terminal-Bench 2.183.8%strong check: it runs or it doesn't
PaperBench21.0%no check
SciCode4.6%weak check: hand-written tests

A twenty-fold spread on how good the check is. Coding agents work because pytest exists. SciCode is not check-free, since it has hand-written tests, and it still lands at 4.6%. That is why the rule is a bound and not an equality: a weak check caps you low, and a strong one only stops you being capped.

Now place tutoring on that scale. It sits in the bottom half, and the reason is measured rather than argued:

Across 223 real tutoring domains, no model beat chance at labelling an incorrect student action.

Not "performed poorly." Chance. The most basic operation a tutor performs — look at what a learner did and say what is wrong with it — is currently unverifiable by the systems being sold to do it.

4. What that means commercially

1

Every AI-tutoring capability is downstream of the verifier. Adaptive pivoting needs to know the learner is wrong and how. Generate-and-select needs a selector, and selection by test measures +8.14pp against −3.20pp for selection by LLM judge, an eleven-point spread in the wrong direction for the judge. A teachable agent needs its tutee's downstream accuracy as ground truth. None of it works without the instrument.

2

The field is currently optimising against two instruments that do not work. The leading agentic-education systems are tuned against LLM-simulated students and scored by LLM-as-judge. Simulated students show near-zero misconception faithfulness across seven models. They do not hold the belief they are role-playing. And the judge is worse than not selecting at all.

3

Nobody can currently prove efficacy, which means nobody can win procurement on it. A census across 20 education-AI subfields found 2,907 arXiv papers, at most 1.79% carrying any learning-outcome marker, and eight subfields at zero. Search ERIC and you get 1,565 records on ChatGPT in education against seven randomised trials, three of them second-language learning. The first company that can demonstrate a delayed unassisted outcome is not competing on features.

5. Why humans-in-the-loop is the wedge, not the cost line

The usual framing treats human tutors as the expensive part to be automated away. The evidence says the opposite, and it comes from the only trial that measured a human-supervised AI tutor at scale.

Tutors accepted 74.4% of 3,617 AI-generated drafts unedited, a figure that verifies exactly (2,691/3,617, zero harmful messages, five factual errors). It is usually quoted as an efficiency win, and that reading is not supported: the authors state their design "precludes a rigorous measurement of throughput or efficiency." The published throughput gain comes from a six-tutor role-play simulation, and the labour rate underpinning its saving is cited to a tutoring marketplace's blog post.

So take the acceptance rate as what it is, a measured signal stream rather than a measured productivity gain. The interesting number is the other one:

44.3% of tutor edits were slowing the AI's questioning down before it exasperated the student.

Read what that describes. The human was not writing the content. The human was acting as the verifier, catching the cases where the model's pedagogical judgement was wrong, in a domain where no automated check exists.

→

A human-in-the-loop tutoring operation is a labelled-data engine for the instrument the category is missing. Every accept, every edit, and every rejection is a labelled judgement about whether a pedagogical action was correct, and so a training signal for a step-level verifier, generated as a by-product of delivering the service.

The humans are not the cost to be removed. They are the reason the asset compounds. A pure-AI competitor has no mechanism to generate this data. A pure-human competitor generates it and does not capture it.

6. The Cursor pattern, and where it breaks in education

The closest template is what happened in coding. It transfers in three places and breaks in one, and the break decides the strategy.

What the pattern actually is

1

Wrap the frontier model to get distribution. No proprietary model at the start. The product is the surface, not the intelligence.

2

Instrument the inner loop, not the chat. The defensible signal was never the conversation. It was accept · reject · edit on a suggestion, fired hundreds of times an hour, by every user, for free.

3

Train the narrow model the labs are not optimising for. Low-latency completion is a different task from reasoning, and frontier labs deprioritise it. Owning a task the labs do not want is the only kind of model advantage that survives their next release.

4

A better base model becomes an input, not a threat.

OBSERVED — the pattern is inferred from public product behaviour, not from disclosed internals. Treat the mechanism as the claim; the company is the illustration.

The inner loop, the narrow task, the deprioritised model

Tutoring has the same inner loop and nobody is capturing it. The trial in §5 supplies both halves: 74.4% accepted, and 44.3% of edits slowing the questioning down. That is accept · reject · edit, on a pedagogical decision, generated by the act of doing the job.

The narrow task is equally clear. Not lesson planning, not curriculum, not the chat: step-level error diagnosis and next-move selection. High frequency, low latency, narrow, and nowhere near solved by the labs, which sit at chance across 223 domains.

The part that does not transfer, and it decides everything

In coding, the accept signal is validated within seconds. In tutoring, you find out in six weeks.

A developer accepts a completion and the code compiles, the tests pass or fail, the thing runs. The label is cheap to collect and immediately grounded, which is the same fact as pytest existing, arriving from the other direction.

A tutor accepts a draft. Was it the right pedagogical move? You cannot know from the acceptance. Accepting is a judgement about plausibility, and plausibility is the signal this survey shows to be unreliable. Felt learning and real learning move in opposite directions, preference shifting at d ≈ 0.48 while knowledge does not move at all.

So an education company copying the pattern naively builds a very large corpus of what looked right to a tutor at the time. That is a fluency model. It is not a verifier, and it will confidently reproduce the field's existing errors at scale.

The delayed test has to be a product feature

→

You have to close the loop to outcome, and it has to be inside the product. The accept/reject stream supplies volume. A delayed, unassisted, novel-item check supplies truth. Neither is sufficient; the pairing is the asset.

That is a product decision, not a research decision. It means shipping the delayed test as a routine feature rather than running it once for a white paper. It costs conversion, and it is the only thing that converts a plausibility corpus into a verifier.

And that is what makes it hard enough to be defensible. Everyone in the category already measures satisfaction and completion. Almost nobody measures anything after the tool is taken away. ERIC returns zero records for "retention test" AND "ChatGPT". A competitor cannot casually add this later, because the label only counts if it was attached to the decision at the time it was made.

Lower volume, and a contested premise

Volume is an order of magnitude lower. A completion fires hundreds of times an hour; a pedagogical decision fires perhaps twenty to sixty. The corpus accumulates roughly 10× slower per user-hour, which changes the scale at which the model becomes good and therefore the funding profile.

And the data-moat thesis is contested even in coding. The competing account is that distribution and product craft did the work and the model followed. INFERENCE, and we have not found it settled either way. Assume it is at least partly true here: the verifier is the durable asset, and it will not save a product nobody uses.

7. The economics, corrected: why "at token cost" is the wrong frame

Inference is 0.43% of the delivered cost of a human-supervised tutoring session. Falling token prices are economically irrelevant to this P&L.

Two independent measurements converge on it. A published cost model puts tokens at £0.0037 of a £0.861 session. And a deployed programme's measured API bill was $1,419.66 for 429 tutors over two months, or $19.86 per tutor per year, against a tutor working 200 hours at the US mean tutor wage. Same number, from two directions.

That retires a category of pitch. "Our costs fall as models get cheaper" is true and worth 0.43%. The entire margin question is the leverage ratio — how many learner-hours one paid tutor-hour can supervise — and nobody has measured it.

The one audited comparable went the wrong way

A listed tutoring company rebuilt, in its own words, "on entirely new, AI-native codebases." In that year its gross margin moved 67.5% → 58.0%, with expert costs up $5.2M on revenue down $11.2M. The following quarter recovered to 66.2%, on price rises and expert incentives, not on AI. Its own 10-K states:

"There can be no assurance that our investments in AI will be beneficial to our business."

That is the only audited evidence of an AI-native rebuild in this exact business model, and it is negative. It does not mean the thesis is wrong. The leverage ratio has to be reported, monthly, by anyone claiming it.

The relief money never arrived, and the TAM has no source

1

Summed from the education department's own state-level data, the reported tutoring line of the pandemic relief appropriation is $994.7M, 0.52% of $189.5bn. The funding wave never arrived, and anyone modelling a district-funded tutoring market on it is modelling something that did not happen.

2

Traceability on the tutoring TAM fails at six of six analyst houses. Two dead links, one report not shown to exist, one figure internally inconsistent by three orders of magnitude. And the US has not measured per-pupil special-education spending since 1999–2000. The numbers in the deck you are shown mostly do not have a source behind them.

9. The recursive loop is the same asset again

The deep-tech version of this is a system that improves its own instruction from its own outcome data. It is the most valuable unbuilt thing in the field, and the reason nobody has it is not ambition.

Self-improvement optimisers have spent three years maximising benchmark accuracy. Instructional-policy research closed the loop on real human retention with 2014 machinery. The two literatures have never met.

Six arXiv queries and ten ERIC queries confirm it: zero optimiser-in-the-loop trials on human learners. Meanwhile a model-based personalised scheduler, deployed for a full semester in a middle-school course, produced +16.5% retention with the machinery of 2014.

The engineering problem, stated exactly

An optimiser needs thousands of fitness evaluations. A delayed unassisted retention test yields one evaluation per learner per six weeks, with enormous variance. That gap — not compute, not model quality — is why the loop does not exist.

A three-tier fitness function follows, and the middle tier is this page's subject:

TierSignalLatencyVolumeTrustworthy?
FastTutor accept / reject / editSecondsEnormousNo, it measures plausibility
SurrogateThe verifierSecondsEnormousOnly if calibrated against truth
TruthDelayed, unassisted, novel itemWeeksSparseYes

The recursive loop is what the verifier unlocks, not a second play alongside it. The verifier is the surrogate fitness function, the only thing that can stand in for a six-week outcome at the frequency an optimiser needs. Build it and the loop follows; skip it and you are optimising plausibility at scale, which is the failure mode already documented in the field's leading systems.

10. Auto-research: one half is done, the other is not automatable

Reading is solved well enough to rely on. PaperQA2 matches or exceeds subject-matter experts on literature synthesis, with 70% of flagged contradictions validated. This survey is an existence proof of the same thing at scale: dozens of research reports, primary sources throughout, and every correction published in an append-only ledger, a substantial minority of them found by adversarial review rather than by the authors.

Generating new evidence is not. It needs learners, consent, and elapsed time. And there is a constraint people underestimate: across 16 studies and 5,873 participants, people object to being experimented on even when they find either arm individually acceptable, and the effect is undiminished among professionals.

So auto-research accelerates the reading and the design of experiments, and cannot accelerate their running. The realistic claim is a system that continuously re-derives what is known, flags where the corpus contradicts itself, and proposes the next experiment. That is worth a great deal and is not the same as a self-improving tutor.

11. Video-in: there is a legal wall, and it is in the wrong place

Prohibited, not merely regulated

EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) bans emotion inference in education. Not high-risk but prohibited, and in force since February 2025. Reading a student's face to infer frustration, confusion or engagement is the one thing on this list that is illegal rather than difficult.

Article 3(39) restricts the ban to biometric-derived inference, which leaves clickstream-based affect detection — the dominant technique in the research literature — in a grey zone. That is the highest-value unresolved legal question in the field.

And it would be the wrong thing to build anyway. Pedagogical avatars measure g ≈ 0.19, and three 2024 field experiments improved affect at d = .85–1.01 while learning did not move at all. Emotion-responsive tutoring optimises the felt axis, the one this survey shows runs opposite to the real one.

What video-in is genuinely for

1

Watching the work, not the face. Answer-position recognition on student work reaches 98.4%. One mandatory caveat: vision-language models silently repair student errors while transcribing them, returning the corrected work because that is what training rewards. The mistakes are the entire reason for looking. Transcription must be constrained to a verbatim, error-preserving task and checked against exactly that.

2

Watching gesture, which is the unexploited one. In a three-arm trial teaching the same strategy as physical action, concrete gesture and abstract gesture, only gesture transferred. Handling the objects produced learning that stayed stuck to the objects.

Gesture is representational compression, having already thrown away the specific instance and kept the structure. So a camera watching a learner's hands is watching the leading indicator of transfer, weeks before a test could show it. It is legal, it is grounded in the one result that reverses the manipulatives intuition, and nobody is building it.

Video-out

The substrate is real. A generated world runs at 640×368 and 25 FPS with roughly 200 ms of model-side latency, which fits inside a human conversational turn-gap, and it carries persistent world state alongside a separate event stream. The limit is equally real: generated worlds get physics wrong, at 39.6% adherence in the best case and 22% on the hard subset. Keep the world generative and the physics symbolic.

12. Gamification: the moderators point away from what everyone ships

Gamification's cognitive effect is real and survives a high-rigour subsplit. The claim vendors actually make — it keeps people coming back — rests on the weakest and least stable cell in the table: behavioural g = .25, with the confidence interval's lower bound at .04.

And the moderator analysis points somewhere else entirely:

The significant moderators are game fiction and social interaction, with combining competition and collaboration particularly effective. Nothing supports points, badges or leaderboards as the active ingredient.

Three further results that should govern any roadmap here:

a

Novelty decay is measured, not folklore. Perceived benefit declines with time on the service.

b

Even theory-driven design dips. A deliberately need-supporting implementation, measured four times across 15 weeks, produced a curvilinear autonomous motivation curve, an initial downward trend that only later recovered. The companion paper is titled "Unravelling the ambivalent motivational power of gamification."

c

The undermining effect is specific. Across 128 experiments: tangible, expected, performance-contingent rewards significantly undermine intrinsic motivation. That is the exact description of an XP bar.

So the buildable version is fiction and genuine social structure, never a points economy. And no engagement metric may serve as an objective, because streaks, XP, DAU and time-on-app all pass the Null-Learner Test, meaning an agent that teaches nothing can max them out.

13. The wedge: high-stakes exams are the one place the loop closes for free

Everything above says the hard part is the third tier of the fitness function, a delayed, unassisted, novel-item outcome. It is slow, sparse, expensive, and it costs conversion to collect.

In competitive-exam preparation, somebody else administers it, and the student wants you to have the result.

SAT · PSAT · JEE · NEET · GATE · EAMCET · gaokao. Proctored. Novel items each cycle. Months after study. Scored, ranked, and reported.

Read that against the requirements. Proctored means genuinely unassisted, the condition almost no edtech study achieves. New paper each cycle means novel items, not the ones practised. Months later means delayed. And the score comes back because the candidate cares about it more than you do.

The truth signal that costs everyone else a research programme is, in this segment, the customer's own objective.

Cold start, counterfactual, motivation, syllabus

1

The verifier's cold-start problem is smallest here. Past papers with published keys, official rubrics, and — for the big exams — decades of item-level response data. A closed syllabus and a definitive answer key is the nearest thing education has to pytest.

2

The counterfactual is bad, which is where returns are largest. This survey's reading of three trials together: the measured return on pedagogical design scales with how bad the counterfactual was. Against a competent chatbot in a well-resourced classroom, designed pedagogy has not yet demonstrated an advantage. Against an overcrowded coaching batch, or nothing, it does.

3

The motivation problem is solved by the exam, not by you. That matters more than it sounds, because §12 shows that the engagement machinery everyone ships is the weakest cell in its own evidence base. An externally imposed, dated, high-stakes goal removes the need for a points economy, the thing that measurably undermines intrinsic motivation.

4

Expertise reversal is per-topic, and a syllabus is a topic list. The one aptitude–treatment interaction that survived fifty years of searching needs a per-topic prior knowledge estimate. A closed, enumerated syllabus makes that measurable in 15–40 seconds per topic rather than being a modelling problem.

14. Publishers hold the asset, and it is not the content

The instinct is that a publisher brings distribution and curriculum alignment. Both true and both replaceable. The asset that is not replaceable is buried in the psychometrics:

An item bank was never valuable because it held items. It was valuable because it held calibrated items. Generation drove item-production cost to zero and left calibration cost untouched.

Automatic item generation reaches break-even at 173–247 items per fine-grained content area, and that is per area, not per programme. Producing questions is free now. Knowing an item's difficulty, its discrimination, and which misconception each distractor actually catches is not, and it requires response data from real students at scale.

A textbook publisher with decades of item-level response data is sitting on the calibration corpus that a verifier needs, in a form nobody else can assemble, and most of them are valuing themselves on the content instead. The deal to do is for the response data, not the pages.

The integration is also the least threatening thing a publisher can be sold. Not a platform migration and not a replacement but an addendum: the same book, with the practice loop instrumented and the diagnosis attached. It preserves their product and their channel, and it produces the data that makes the next edition genuinely adaptive.

15. What the market evidence says before you commit

Three findings that should change the plan, not just caveat it

China is a policy risk, not a market. The 2021 double reduction order — retrieved in full from the Ministry of Education — states that no new approvals will be issued for core-subject tutoring institutions and that existing ones re-register as non-profits. One listed operator's revenue fell 62% in twelve months, per its filing. Gaokao preparation is not an addressable commercial market under current policy.

The market-size figures you will be shown do not exist. No official Chinese statistic on tutoring market size could be located, before or after 2021. Every "$100bn+ market" number in circulation descends from vendor reports rather than from a national statistics office. The destroyed value is itself unmeasurable, which should be sobering about the downside and not only the upside.

And this segment is maximally positional. Exam preparation buys rank. Every effect size in this survey was measured where the treatment group had something the control group did not. If a tool that lifts everyone by 0.4 SD reaches everyone, the absolute learning is real and the rank is unchanged. That does not break the business in the short run. It does mean the durable claim has to be about capability, and the company should know which one it is selling.

16. The product: every book comes with a live mentor

The book stays the book. What changes is the substrate underneath it.

The Kindle comparison repays taking literally, because Kindle did not win on the reading experience. The device was commoditised deliberately. What Amazon actually built was an account, a sync layer, and a telemetry stream, and publishers kept publishing throughout.

The channel, the asset, and the closed loop

1

The publisher keeps their product and their channel, which is what makes the deal signable. They are not being platformed; they are being instrumented.

2

The mentor is the surface; the learner model is the asset. Same structure as the device being sold at cost while the account is the moat. Give away the conversation. Own the state and the verifier.

3

And in exam prep the loop closes. Aggregate misconception data supplies the volume, the verifier supplies the surrogate signal, and the exam supplies the truth. It is proctored, novel, delayed, and reported back because the candidate wants it reported. That is the three-tier fitness function from §9, fully instantiated, in one product.

Disintermediation, and correlated error

Both from this survey's own findings

Publishers eventually fought Amazon. A partner who comes to feel disintermediated will litigate the pricing and take the channel back. The deal structure has to leave the publisher owning something that compounds too, which is an argument for giving them the calibration outputs on their own catalogue, not just a revenue share.

And one live mentor across every book is this survey's named unstudied risk. Thirty teachers make thirty different mistakes, and the system averages them out. A single mentor's errors are systematic and identical for every reader simultaneously, what the survey calls correlated pedagogical error, for which there is currently no monitoring and no name in the literature. Shipping this product at scale is the experiment. The mitigation is the same instrument as the moat: an ensemble of independent verifiers, treating disagreement in the explanation rather than the answer as the alarm.

17. Why it has to be the whole ecosystem, and not one publisher

The argument is structural rather than about scale, and it follows from what the asset actually is.

A misconception belongs to the concept, not to the container. The same error in integration by parts appears in a textbook, a video, and an author's newsletter, and it is the same error.

The verifier trains on concepts and the errors people make against them, not on anyone's pages. The consequence is sharp: no single publisher can build this, because no single publisher can see enough of the concept. They observe their own catalogue's slice of a shared error space. A neutral layer observes the space.

That is the platform position, derived rather than asserted, and it is why breadth of the ecosystem matters more than the size of any one partner. How much more is unmeasured.

Sequence by procurement friction, not by partner size

The instinct is to sign the biggest publisher first. The evidence says start at the other end, because what binds in this market is the gate rather than the appetite.

Start where friction is lowest and data velocity is highest. The corpus you build with authors is the reason the publishers eventually sign. You arrive with the calibration their own catalogue lacks, rather than asking them to fund its creation.

The glue nobody has built, named twice by this survey's own research

Surveying what a school could actually self-host today, the frontier sweep found four holes between a maintained open stack and a deployable system. The fourth is this one, verbatim from that report:

No glue between an LMS and a model. Rosters, gradebooks, assignments, standards alignment — the highest-value unclaimed open project in the field, and it needs no new research at all.

That finding came from the supply side, asking what a school could self-host. The ecosystem argument arrives at the same object from the demand side. Both are workstreams of this project, so this is not independent confirmation. The same team reached one conclusion by two routes, which is weaker evidence, and we say so. What it does establish is that the gap is not an artifact of how the question was framed.

The schema that does not exist

The interchange standards in this space each solve a different piece. One carries rosters and launch, one carries assessment items, one carries learning events. None of them represents a misconception. There is no standard way to say this learner holds this specific incorrect model of this concept, and here is the evidence.

Whoever defines that representation defines how the ecosystem exchanges its most valuable object, and publishing it openly is what makes a neutral layer trustworthy enough for competitors to route through, which is an INFERENCE rather than an observation.

Neutrality is the product, and it has to have teeth

The commitment that makes the data flow

Never publish content. The moment the layer competes with a partner's catalogue, the data stops, and every partner is simultaneously evaluating whether you are their infrastructure or their successor. Amazon eventually became a publisher, and publishers eventually fought Amazon.

This costs something real: content is the higher-margin business and you are declining it permanently. That is the price of being routed through rather than around, and it should be stated in the first conversation with every partner rather than discovered later.

The second risk is a frontier lab doing this directly. They have the distribution and the model. What they do not have is the partner relationships or the appetite to sit beneath somebody else's brand, which is why the neutral position, and only the neutral position, is defensible against them.

18. What makes it hard, which is the point

Three things make it hard, and all three are surmountable by an operator rather than by a lab:

a

The labels must come from real student work, in the wild. Simulated students have near-zero misconception faithfulness. You cannot synthesise this corpus.

b

Judgement of a pedagogical action is not a single ground truth. It needs the tutor's decision, the learner's subsequent behaviour, and a delayed outcome, which means you need the delivery relationship, not just the transcripts.

c

Decades of labelled intelligent-tutoring logs already exist and are public. The cold-start is smaller than it looks, and the public testbed is sitting there unused.

19. The three questions to ask any competitor

1

"Show me the delayed, unassisted, novel-item outcome." Not the practice score, not the same items. Weeks later, device closed. ERIC returns zero records for "retention test" AND "ChatGPT". Without it, nothing was measured about learning.

2

"What was your control condition?" If it was nothing, the result is uninformative, because against nothing almost everything works. The bar is plain ChatGPT, because that is what a learner has for free.

3

"What happened to the gap, not the mean?" Untargeted delivery reliably loads on already-strong learners. A healthy mean concealing a widened gap has made things worse.

20. What would kill this thesis

Stated in advance, so it can be checked

A frontier release that labels student errors reliably. If a general model crosses from chance to competent at step-level error diagnosis, the verifier stops being a proprietary asset and becomes an API call. This is the single largest risk and it is not remote.

The counterfactual result generalising. In a controlled trial, two designed pedagogical interventions produced no significant advantage over plain ChatGPT. One reading of the corpus as a whole is that returns on pedagogical design scale with how bad the counterfactual was, largest where instruction is scarce and near zero in a well-resourced classroom. If that holds, the addressable market is not where the money is.

The prior on the adjacent claim is bad. Four separate attempts to reduce special-education administrative burden — computerisation, delegation, deregulation, and a statutory waiver authority — were measured, and all four returned zero. The AI claim is the fifth attempt, and its entire measured base is one RCT of 22 novice teachers.

21. The one-line version

Everyone is building the tutor. The bottleneck is the examiner, and a human-in-the-loop operation is the only structure that produces one while it trades.

The product that does it: every book comes with a live mentor. The publisher keeps the book. The mentor is given away. The learner model and the verifier are kept, and in exam preparation the examiner that validates them is one somebody else already administers.
